Segment Customers with K-Means Clustering in Python

Turn a real e-commerce order history into RFM features (recency, frequency, monetary value), scale them correctly, and cluster customers with scikit-learn's KMeans — then translate the four resulting clusters into segments a marketing team could actually act on.

Mehdi Lotfinejad
August 9, 2026 · 12 min read

A marketing team wants to send a win-back discount to customers who are drifting away — but “drifting away” isn’t a column in any database. What they actually have is an order history: timestamps, order IDs, amounts. Turning that raw history into a handful of groups you can act on — this segment gets a discount, that one gets a loyalty perk, this one gets left alone — is exactly the kind of problem K-Means clustering was built for. It’s the same algorithm behind finding an image’s dominant colors, just pointed at a different kind of point: not a pixel’s RGB values, but a customer’s recency, frequency, and spend.

To segment customers with K-Means, first turn every customer’s order history into three numbers — how many days since their last order, how many orders they’ve placed, and how much they’ve spent in total — then scale those numbers so no single one dominates, fit scikit-learn’s KMeans on the result, and read each finished cluster’s center back as a group’s typical behavior. The only real trap is skipping the scaling step (or worse, skipping a log transform first): raw spending figures are so skewed that an untreated fit ends up clustering almost entirely on a handful of big spenders instead of on actual buying patterns.

Distance Only Understands Numbers

K-Means has no idea what a “customer” is. It only understands points in space and the distance between them — the same as when it’s grouping pixels by RGB value, except this time each point has to be built by hand, because “days since last order” and “total pounds spent” don’t arrive already living on the same axis the way a pixel’s red, green, and blue channels do.

The standard set of axes for this problem is called RFM: Recency (how long since the customer last bought something), Frequency (how many separate orders they’ve placed), and Monetary value (how much they’ve spent in total). Three columns, one row per customer, and now every customer is a point in a 3D space where “close together” means “behaves similarly” — the entire premise clustering runs on. The catch, worked through in the next two sections, is that recency, frequency, and monetary value don’t naturally share a scale: one lives in days, one is a small integer, and one can run into the tens of thousands. Left alone, K-Means’s distance calculation will happily let the biggest-magnitude column drown out the other two.

A Real Order History, Reduced to One Row per Customer

Instead of generating anything synthetic, this post works from a real one: the “Online Retail” transaction log of a UK-based online gift retailer, roughly 540,000 line items between December 2010 and December 2011, hosted at the UCI Machine Learning Repository under a CC BY 4.0 license. The raw log is one row per line item, which isn’t yet useful for segmentation — you need one row per customer. Aggregating it takes three steps: drop rows with no customer ID or a cancelled order, pick a snapshot date (one day after the last recorded order in the log), then for every customer compute Recency (days between their last order and the snapshot), Frequency (their number of distinct orders), and Monetary (the sum of every line item’s quantity times price). That aggregation is a one-time step done ahead of this post; what you load here is the resulting table — 4,338 rows, one per customer.

Data: aggregated from the Online Retail dataset (Chen, 2015), UCI Machine Learning Repository (CC BY 4.0), reduced from roughly 540,000 line items down to one Recency/Frequency/Monetary row per customer.

Customer 12346 makes the point on the very first row: 326 days since their one and only order, worth £77,183.60 — simultaneously your biggest spender and someone who hasn’t been back in almost a year. describe() on the whole table confirms this isn’t a one-off:

The median customer spent £674.48 across two orders. The maximum spent £280,206.02 — over 400 times the median, on a column whose mean (£2,054.27) is already three times its own median. That gap between mean and median is the fingerprint of a heavily skewed distribution, and skew() puts a number on it:

A skew of zero is a symmetric distribution. Recency’s 1.25 is mildly lopsided, which makes sense — plenty of customers ordered fairly recently, and a long tail didn’t. Frequency and Monetary, at 12 and 19, are not mild: a handful of extreme customers are stretching those two columns far past where the bulk of the data actually sits.

What Skipping the Log Transform Actually Costs You

It’s tempting to jump straight to StandardScaler and KMeans — scaling is the fix for mismatched units, after all, and units are exactly what’s different across Recency, Frequency, and Monetary. Try it directly on the raw numbers first, though, so you can see why that’s not the whole fix:

Four clusters were requested and four came back, but look at their sizes — one of them has 13 customers in it. Grouping the naive fit’s output by cluster and checking spending explains why:

StandardScaler did its job on paper — it made every column mean-zero, unit-variance — but variance itself is dominated by a few huge outliers when a column is this skewed, so the scaled distances are still almost entirely about how much someone spent. KMeans obediently carved the data into “spent an enormous amount” (13 people), “spent a lot” (204 people), and two much larger groups that barely differ on recency or frequency at all. That’s not four customer personas; it’s four spending brackets.

The standard fix is to compress the skew before scaling, with a log transform:

np.log1p computes log(1 + x) rather than plain log(x), which matters here because Frequency can legitimately be a small integer and plain log(0) is undefined. Every skew figure drops sharply — Monetary goes from 19.32 to 0.39, Frequency from 12.07 to 1.21 — because a log scale treats “£100 to £1,000” and “£10,000 to £100,000” as the same size of jump, which matches how spending actually varies across a real customer base far better than treating raw pounds as evenly spaced.

Choosing k With the Elbow, Then a Second Opinion

With the skew under control, scale the log-transformed features and you get the input KMeans should actually see:

Every feature now has mean zero and standard deviation one, so no single column can dominate a distance calculation just by having bigger raw numbers. The last open question is k — how many segments to ask for. Our post on clustering an image’s dominant colors got to skip this by knowing the answer in advance — a banner drawn with four colors. A real customer base doesn’t hand you that shortcut, which is exactly the case the elbow method lesson in our Machine Learning course covers. Fit across a range of k and track inertia_, the sum of squared distances from each point to its assigned center:

Inertia always falls as k grows, so the real question is never whether adding a cluster helps — it’s whether it helps enough to be worth it. The drop from k=1 to k=2 is roughly 50%, and k=2 to k=3 is still a healthy 25%. By k=4 it’s down to 19%, and every step after that saves less than the one before: a real but softening elbow, not the sharp corner a textbook diagram promises. Because real behavioral data rarely gives a clean, unarguable answer, it’s worth checking a second metric before committing:

Silhouette score measures how much closer each point sits to its own cluster than to the next-nearest one, from -1 (wrong cluster) up to 1 (perfectly separated). Real customer data rarely gets close to 1, and 0.337 is a normal, workable score for behavioral clustering rather than a red flag. k=3 and k=4 are tied here, and k=5 is worse, so the elbow and the silhouette score agree on stopping at 4 rather than pushing further — and four segments, rather than three, leaves enough room to separate “new” customers from “lapsed” ones, which turns out to matter once you see what each group actually looks like.

Fitting KMeans and Naming the Segments

Refitting KMeans at k=4 and reading the result back onto the original table is the whole payoff — the same fit/predict shape documented in scikit-learn’s clustering user guide, and covered in more depth in the K-Means with Scikit-Learn lesson:

Those integer labels don’t mean anything on their own — KMeans assigns 0 through 3 in whatever order it happens to settle on, not by any ranking. Grouping the original, un-logged RFM columns by cluster is what turns four arbitrary numbers into four readable customer profiles:

Read as a table these are just numbers, but the pattern names itself. Cluster 1 (716 customers) ordered 12 days ago on average, has placed nearly 14 orders, and has spent over £8,000 — Champions. Cluster 2 (1,173 customers) is the largest reliably active group, ordering every couple of months at moderate spend — call them Core. Cluster 0 (837 customers) ordered recently but has only 1–2 orders on the books — New or Occasional, too early to tell if they’ll become Core. Cluster 3, the biggest group at 1,612 customers, hasn’t ordered in six months on average and has only ever placed one or two orders — At Risk, and the group a win-back campaign should actually target instead of guessing.

Where This Breaks If You’re Not Careful

Cluster ID numbers are not stable across runs. Refit the same k=4 model with a different random_state and check where the highest-spending group lands:

The Champions group still lands on label 1 here — coincidence — but Core and New/Occasional have swapped places, showing up as 3 and 2 instead of 2 and 0. (Our post on K-Means for dominant colors covers a related random_state/n_init gotcha; this is the same underlying instability, showing up as label reshuffling instead of a worse fit.) The customers themselves land in essentially the same real-world groups either way — but any code that hardcodes cluster == 1 to mean “Champions” will silently break the next time the model gets refit. Always re-derive the label from the group’s actual characteristics, the way the profile table above does, never from the raw integer.

The snapshot date has to be deliberate, not “today.” Recency is snapshot_date minus each customer's last order date, and this dataset’s own last recorded order is from December 2011. Computing recency against today’s date instead of the data’s own most recent date would add well over a decade to every customer’s recency figure, swamping any real signal in the feature entirely. The rule is simple but easy to get backwards in a live pipeline: the snapshot date is always the most recent point the data itself actually covers, not the day you happen to be running the script.

A silhouette score of 0.337 is good enough, not great — and that’s expected. K-Means assumes clusters are roughly round and similarly sized in feature space; real customer behavior is fuzzier than that; a handful of Core customers genuinely sit close to the boundary with New/Occasional or Champions. Treat these four segments as a strong starting point for a marketing team’s judgment, not as ground truth handed down by the algorithm — and recompute them periodically as behavior shifts, rather than treating one fit as permanent.

None of this required knowing anything about these customers beyond three numbers pulled from an order log, and that’s the real value of clustering over hand-built segment rules: it finds the groups your data already contains, instead of groups you guessed at in advance.
