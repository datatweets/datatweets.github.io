Estimate Probability in Python with Monte Carlo Simulation

You don't need a combinatorics formula to know how likely something is — you can just simulate it thousands of times and count. This post estimates the classic birthday paradox both ways in Python, then measures exactly how many trials it takes for the simulated answer to actually match the exact one.

Mehdi Lotfinejad
August 8, 2026 · 8 min read

Ask whether two people in a room of 23 share a birthday, and most people guess low — 23 out of 365 feels like long odds. The real answer is just over 50%, a coin flip. You can get that number two ways: work out the exact formula, or just simulate 23 random birthdays a few thousand times and count how often two match. This post does both, in Python, and then answers the question a formula can’t: simulate it once and you might get 0.51, simulate it again and you might get 0.48 — so how many trials does it actually take before you can trust the number you got?

The short answer: with numpy’s random generator, a few thousand trials gets you within about a percentage point of the exact value for a problem like this one, and the gap keeps shrinking (roughly by a factor of the square root of your trial count) the more trials you run — that’s the law of large numbers doing its job. What it doesn’t do automatically is tell you when you’ve run enough; that’s a judgment call you make by watching the estimate stabilize, which is exactly what the rest of this post walks through.

Two Ways to Get the Same Number

Every probability question in this post has two independent routes to an answer, and the interesting part is watching them agree:

  • Exact: work out the probability directly from the structure of the problem — count the outcomes, or use a known formula. No randomness involved; the answer is a single fixed number.
  • Simulated: run the random process itself, over and over, and calculate what fraction of runs produced the outcome you’re asking about. The answer is itself a random variable — a different run gives a (slightly) different number.

The exact route is the one to trust when it’s available — it doesn’t have sampling noise. The simulated route is the one you reach for when the exact math is hard, unknown, or you just want a sanity check on a formula you’re not confident you derived correctly. This post deliberately picks a problem where both are easy, so you can watch the simulated numbers close in on the exact one instead of taking either on faith.

The Birthday Paradox, Computed Exactly

With n people in a room (nobody’s birthday is February 29th, and every day is equally likely), the easiest way to compute “probability at least two share a birthday” is through its complement — the probability that all n birthdays are different — because that one has a clean, direct formula:

Each factor in the loop is “given the first i people already have distinct birthdays, what fraction of days are still free for the next person” — the first person can’t clash with anyone (365/365), the second has 364/365 free days left, and so on down to the n-th. Multiply those together and you have the probability of zero clashes; subtract from 1 and you have the probability of at least one. By n = 23, that probability has already crossed 50% — half of all 23-person rooms have a shared birthday, which is the surprising part people usually mean when they call this a “paradox.” It isn’t actually a paradox, just a number that grows faster than intuition expects.

All three estimates land within about half a percentage point of the exact value, with a single fixed np.random.default_rng(42) seed — which is a reasonable place to stop if you just wanted a rough check, but it’s worth being honest about what “within half a point” actually depends on: how many trials you ran.

How Many Trials Is Actually Enough?

Fix n = 23 and rerun the same simulation with more and more trials, holding everything else constant:

Ten trials gets you a wildly unreliable 0.60 — with only 10 simulated rooms, one extra-lucky or extra-unlucky draw swings the estimate by a lot. The error drops fast at first (100 trials roughly quarters it) and then keeps dropping, but with diminishing returns — going from 10,000 to 100,000 trials (10x the work) only takes the error from 0.0082 to 0.0007. That’s the law of large numbers in its practical, “how much compute do I actually need” form: more trials always help, but each additional order of magnitude buys you roughly one more digit of precision, not a proportional improvement.

One Run Isn’t Enough Evidence — Run It Several Times

Everything above used a single seed per trial count, which hides something important: at a small trial count, which seed you happen to pick matters almost as much as how many trials you ran. Run the same 500-trial simulation with five different seeds:

Five honest runs of the identical experiment, same code, same trial count, spread across a 0.05-wide range — nearly a 10% relative swing on a number that’s supposed to answer one fixed question. Bump the trial count to 50,000 and rerun the same five-seed check, changing nothing else:

The spread across seeds tightens from 0.05 wide down to under 0.003 wide, and every one of the five now agrees with the exact value to two decimal places. This is the real lesson, and it’s easy to miss if you only ever run a simulation once: a single simulated estimate, however precise it looks printed to four decimal places, doesn’t tell you anything about how much it might have varied with a different seed. If a result matters, rerun it with a few different seeds (or compute a proper confidence interval) before trusting the number — one lucky-looking run at a low trial count is indistinguishable, from the inside, from a well-converged one.

Where This Breaks Down

A fixed seed makes a single run reproducible, not accurate. np.random.default_rng(42) guarantees you and a colleague get the identical 0.5128 if you both run the n=23, 20,000-trial version — reproducibility is genuinely valuable for debugging and sharing results — but it says nothing about how close that number is to the truth. Don’t mistake “I can reproduce this exact number” for “this number is precise.”

Simulation without a known exact answer to check against can’t tell you it’s converged. This post could compare its simulated estimates to the exact formula at every step; most real Monte Carlo problems don’t have that luxury (that’s usually why you’re simulating). The practical substitute is running your simulation at a few different trial counts and seeds, the way this post did, and checking that the answer has stopped moving by more than you care about — if 10,000 trials and 100,000 trials still disagree by more than your tolerance, you don’t have a converged estimate yet, exact-formula check or not.
