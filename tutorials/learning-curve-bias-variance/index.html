Learning Curves in scikit-learn: Diagnose Bias and Variance

A hands-on guide to scikit-learn's learning_curve function: plot training and cross-validation scores against training-set size, then read the shape of the gap to tell whether your model needs more data or a different design.

Mehdi Lotfinejad
August 8, 2026 · 9 min read

Your model’s accuracy came in lower than you hoped, and someone on the team suggests collecting more labeled data. That might fix things. It might also be a few weeks of work that leaves the score exactly where it started, because the real problem was never the amount of data — it was the model. Before you commit to either path, there’s a cheap experiment that tells you which one you’re actually in, and it doesn’t require guessing. (If your model in question is a neural network and the question is when to stop a single training run, that’s a related but different problem covered in diagnosing overfitting with epoch-based learning curves — this post is about the more general question of whether the model needs more rows at all, for any scikit-learn estimator.)

Train the same model on a small slice of your training data, then a bigger slice, then bigger still, scoring it on held-out data at each step. If the training score and the held-out score stay far apart and the held-out score keeps climbing as the slices grow, more data will probably help. If the two scores are already close together and both are stuck at a mediocre number, more data won’t close that gap — you need a more capable model, better features, or both. scikit-learn automates this exact experiment with sklearn.model_selection.learning_curve.

Reading the Gap Instead of Guessing at It

A single train/test score tells you how the model did. It says nothing about why. Two models can post the identical 85% accuracy for completely opposite reasons — one hasn’t seen enough examples yet, the other has seen plenty and simply isn’t flexible enough to do better. A learning curve separates these cases by adding a second axis: instead of one score, you get a whole sequence of scores as the training set grows from small to full size.

Watch two things as that happens: where the training score sits, and how far the held-out score trails behind it.

  • A persistent gap — training score high, held-out score visibly lower, and the held-out score still rising as you add rows — is the signature of high variance. The model is capable enough (it fits the training rows well) but hasn’t seen enough examples to generalize. More data is the fix worth trying first.
  • A closed gap at a low ceiling — both scores converge early and neither one improves much as you add rows — is the signature of high bias. The model has already learned everything it can from this much data; the limitation is its own flexibility, not the row count. More data mostly wastes your time here.

Neither shape is inherently bad — they’re just different diagnoses that call for different fixes. The chart further down in this post shows both shapes side by side, built from the same dataset, so you can see the difference directly rather than taking it on faith.

A Classifier You Can Reproduce: Handwritten Digits

learning_curve needs an estimator, a dataset, and a scoring metric. For the dataset, we’ll use the digits dataset that ships with scikit-learn: 1,797 low-resolution (8×8 pixel) images of handwritten digits 0 through 9, each flattened into 64 grayscale intensity values. It originates from a set of digit forms collected for the UCI Machine Learning Repository and is redistributed with scikit-learn under its BSD license — no download needed beyond pip install scikit-learn.

1,797 images, 64 pixel-intensity features, and a fairly even split across the ten digit classes — between 174 and 183 examples each. (The outputs in this post come from scikit-learn 1.9.0 and numpy 2.5.1.) We’ll classify these with a DecisionTreeClassifier, mainly because its max_depth gives us a direct, easy-to-reason-about dial for model flexibility: shallow trees can’t represent much, unlimited-depth trees can represent almost anything, including noise.

Plotting Your First Learning Curve

learning_curve retrains the estimator from scratch on growing subsets of the training data, using cross-validation at every subset size so each score is an average over multiple splits rather than a single lucky (or unlucky) one. Start with a middling tree — max_depth=8, neither tiny nor unlimited — to see the shape of the output before reading anything into it:

train_sizes=np.linspace(0.1, 1.0, 6) asks for six evenly spaced fractions of the available training data; learning_curve translates those into the actual row counts shown in train_sizes — 143 rows up to 1,437 (the other ~360 rows in each fold are held out for scoring by the cross-validator). train_scores and cv_scores come back as one row per size and one column per cross-validation fold, which is why we average across folds with .mean(axis=1) before reading them.

The pattern here: training accuracy drifts down slightly as the tree sees more (and messier) data, while cross-validation accuracy climbs steadily from 0.557 to 0.843. The gap is shrinking — from 0.44 down to about 0.09 — but it hasn’t closed, and the CV curve is still rising at the last point. That’s an open question worth pinning down with a clearer case on either side, which is exactly what the next two sections do. (The official learning_curve reference documents the full parameter set, including alternate scoring functions and parallelism via n_jobs.)

The Shape of High Variance

Remove the depth limit entirely and the tree can keep splitting until every training leaf holds a single example — the textbook recipe for memorizing rather than learning:

Training accuracy is a flat 1.0 at every single size — an unlimited-depth tree can always carve out a leaf for every training row it’s given, so a perfect training score here proves nothing except that the model has enough capacity to memorize whatever it sees. The number worth watching is the other one: cross-validation accuracy climbs the entire way, from 0.556 up to 0.858, and the gap between the two curves is still 0.142 at the largest size tested. Nothing in this curve says the climb is over. This is the high-variance shape from the mental model above, and it’s the case where going back for more labeled digits would be a reasonable bet.

The Shape of High Bias

Now go the other direction — cap the tree at max_depth=3, so it can only ask three yes/no pixel-intensity questions before it has to commit to an answer:

This is a different animal entirely. By the second size tested, training and cross-validation accuracy are within 0.01–0.09 of each other, and they stay that close for the rest of the run — three yes/no questions just can’t separate ten digit classes well, no matter how many training rows back them up. Both curves are stuck in the low 0.4s to high 0.6s, nowhere near the 0.85+ the unlimited-depth tree reached with the same data. Feeding this model another ten thousand digit images wouldn’t move that plateau; the fix is a model that can represent more structure — more depth, a different algorithm, or richer pixel features — not a bigger training set.

Trusting the Shaded Band

Every score printed above is an average across the five StratifiedKFold splits — worth checking how much they actually vary, since a curve built from noisy averages deserves less confidence than one where the folds agree:

All three models show their widest spread — around 0.07 — at the smallest training size, where a single unlucky fold can swing the average noticeably; the spread settles down to roughly 0.02–0.04 once there’s enough data per fold. If you’re plotting this yourself, drawing a shaded band of mean ± one standard deviation around each curve (rather than just the mean line) makes that narrowing visible and stops you from reading too much into a wiggle at the small-data end. StratifiedKFold specifically — rather than plain KFold — matters here because it keeps each fold’s class proportions close to the full dataset’s; with ten roughly-balanced classes and a plain shuffle-based split, a small training slice could plausibly under-represent one digit by chance. For a large dataset, adding n_jobs=-1 to learning_curve parallelizes the fold-times-size grid of model fits across CPU cores, which is normally the more pressing concern than any of the above.

Where This Breaks in Practice

train_sizes given as fractions must stay inside (0, 1]. It’s an easy typo to make — asking for a size larger than the full dataset doesn’t get silently capped, it raises:

If you want row counts instead of fractions, pass whole numbers greater than 1 (like np.array([200, 500, 1000])) and learning_curve treats the array as absolute sizes rather than proportions.

A perfect training score is a property of the model, not a compliment. The unlimited-depth tree’s flat 1.0 line above isn’t evidence the model has “solved” the problem — for a tree with no depth limit, hitting 1.0 on its own training rows is close to guaranteed. Judge the model by the cross-validation curve and the size of the gap, never by how well it repeats back rows it has already seen.

This diagnoses training-set size, not training time. It’s easy to conflate this curve with the kind that tracks error against training epochs or boosting rounds for a single fit — they look similar and share the name “learning curve,” but they answer different questions. This one holds the model fixed and grows the dataset; the epoch-based version in our neural network overfitting post holds the dataset fixed and grows the number of training passes. Mixing them up means asking “will more data help” when the real question is “when should this specific run stop,” or vice versa.

Reach for learning_curve before you spend a week collecting more labeled data — it turns “maybe more data will help” into a shape you can actually look at.
