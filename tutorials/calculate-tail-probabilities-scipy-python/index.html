How to Calculate Tail Probabilities with SciPy in Python

Fit a gamma distribution to positive repair durations, turn deadlines into lower-tail, upper-tail, and interval probabilities, invert a target probability into a percentile cutoff, and compare every result with unused data.

Mehdi Lotfinejad
August 6, 2026 · 12 min read

A repair team has recorded hundreds of completion times. Its next questions are about decisions, not averages: What share should finish within eight hours? How often will work exceed twelve hours? Which deadline covers 90% of repairs?

To calculate these probabilities in SciPy, fit a suitable continuous distribution, use its cumulative distribution function (cdf) for values at or below a cutoff, and use its survival function (sf) for values above a cutoff. Subtract two CDF values for an interval, and pass a target probability to the percent point function (ppf) to find the matching percentile cutoff.

We will apply this pattern to positive, right-skewed repair durations. More importantly, we will compare the model’s answers with rows that were not used for fitting. That final check shows whether a convenient probability model describes unseen data well enough for the intended decision.

Understanding CDF, SF, and PPF

Let T mean the resolution time for a future repair. It is a continuous random variable: its possible values can include fractions such as 7.25 hours, rather than only whole counts.

A continuous model describes values with a probability density function, or PDF. The density curve shows where outcomes are concentrated. Its height at exactly eight hours is not the probability of an eight-hour repair. Probability is an area across a range of values.

SciPy gives us three more direct tools for decision questions:

The cumulative distribution function, or CDF, returns P(T <= x): the modeled probability that a value is at or below x. The survival function, or SF, returns P(T > x), the remaining upper-tail probability. SciPy defines SF as 1 - CDF, but its gamma distribution reference notes that sf can be more accurate than doing that subtraction yourself.

The percent point function, or PPF, reverses the CDF. Instead of supplying a time and receiving a probability, you supply a lower-tail probability and receive its time cutoff. SciPy’s PPF documentation describes it as the inverse of the CDF.

For a continuous model, including or excluding an exact boundary does not change the theoretical probability because a single point has zero area. In observed, rounded data, boundary choices can change a count. State them explicitly when you perform an empirical check.

Fit a Gamma Model to Positive Durations

Repair times cannot be negative and may have a long right tail: many jobs finish near the main concentration, while a smaller number take much longer. A gamma distribution can represent that shape with positive shape and scale parameters. This is a modeling candidate, not a conclusion that every duration dataset is gamma distributed.

Separate the two saved roles before fitting. Only the 630 fit values may influence the parameters. The 210 check values remain unused until the model and questions are fixed:

Fit the model next. gamma.fit() returns shape, location, and scale estimates. Passing floc=0 fixes the distribution’s lower boundary at zero because this scenario defines zero as the earliest possible repair time:

SciPy’s fit documentation says that the default method is maximum likelihood estimation. In plain English, it searches within the chosen distribution family for parameters that make the observed fitting values most likely. It also warns that the result is not guaranteed to be globally optimal.

floc=0 is an informed constraint, not a standard setting to copy into every problem. A process with a known two-hour minimum may need a location of two. Data that contains valid zeros or negative changes may need another family or transformation.

Calculate the Share That Finishes Within Eight Hours

“Within eight hours” points left from the cutoff, so it is a CDF question. Evaluate the fitted model at 8 and format the result as both a decimal and a percentage:

The fitted model estimates that about 65.0% of comparable repairs finish in eight hours or less. The decimal 0.6497 and percentage 65.0% express the same probability. Do not multiply the value by 100 and then also use Python’s % format.

This is a model estimate for a future value drawn from a similar process. It is not the count of fitting rows below eight hours, and it is not a promise that exactly 65 of the next 100 repairs will meet the cutoff.

Calculate an Upper-Tail Probability with SF

“Longer than twelve hours” points right from the cutoff. Use the survival function so the code states that direction directly:

The fitted upper tail contains about 12.1% of the probability. For ordinary cutoffs, repair_model.sf(12) and 1 - repair_model.cdf(12) should be very close. Prefer sf for a tail question because it communicates the intent and is designed to calculate that tail directly.

An operational label such as “late” still comes from the repair policy, not from the distribution. SciPy calculates the share beyond 12 hours; it does not decide whether 12 hours is an appropriate service target.

Calculate Probability Between Two Times

The CDF through ten hours includes values below the interval. Subtract the CDF through six hours to isolate the probability between the two cutoffs:

The model places about 33.4% of repair times in this four-hour interval. Do not subtract PDF heights at 6 and 10. Heights describe local density; the CDF already contains the area needed for interval probability.

The order also matters. cdf(10) - cdf(6) is non-negative because 10 is the upper boundary. Reversing the values produces a negative number, which cannot be a probability.

Turn a Target Probability into a P90 Cutoff

Now reverse the question. Instead of asking for the probability below a known time, ask which time has 90% of the fitted probability below it. Pass 0.90, not 90, to ppf:

The fitted 90th percentile is 12.64 hours. Under this model, 90% of the probability lies at or below that value and 10% lies above it. Calling the CDF on the returned cutoff is a useful round-trip check: it returns 0.9000, apart from normal floating-point precision.

P90 is a percentile of the modeled duration distribution. It is not a 90% confidence interval for an estimated parameter, and it is not evidence that every future group will achieve 90% coverage.

Check the Fitted Answers on Unused Rows

A fitted curve is an approximation. Compare its answers with simple proportions in the 210 check rows. These rows did not influence the fitted parameters:

The first and third answers are close in this check. The model estimates the within-eight-hour share 1.16 percentage points higher than the observed check share. Its six-to-ten-hour estimate is 0.52 points higher.

The upper tail deserves more attention. The model estimates 12.1% above twelve hours, while 15.2% of the check rows exceed that cutoff. The -3.18 gap means the model estimate is 3.18 percentage points lower than the observed check share. It does not mean that either number is exact for the full process.

Check the percentile in both directions as well. Compare the model cutoff with the empirical p90, then count how many check rows actually fall below the model’s cutoff:

Only 86.2% of the check repairs fall within the model’s 12.64-hour p90. The check sample’s own p90 is 13.50 hours. This result does not make the CDF or PPF calculations wrong. It shows that one fitted gamma curve understates the slow tail in this particular unused sample.

The executed chart puts the density, cumulative directions, and unused-row comparison together:

In the first panel, the histogram shows the fitting rows and the blue line shows the fitted PDF. The orange area, not the curve height at 12, represents the tail probability. In the second panel, the CDF rises toward one while the SF falls toward zero. The last panel makes the model error concrete: a useful approximation can still miss the part of the distribution that matters most to a deadline.

With only 210 check rows, each empirical share also has sampling variation. Use a larger and more representative check set before making a high-impact service decision. If times change by season, repair type, or technician group, check those conditions rather than relying only on one overall result.

Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting

Using pdf(x) as the probability of x. A PDF value is density per unit on the horizontal axis. Use cdf, sf, or an integrated interval when the question asks for a probability.

Using the wrong tail. “At or below” points to cdf. “Above” points to sf. Write the event in symbols such as T > 12 before choosing the method.

Passing percentages on a 0-to-100 scale. ppf expects a probability between 0 and 1. Use 0.90 for p90. repair_model.ppf(90) returns an invalid result because 90 is outside that range.

Fitting before checking the measurement range. A gamma model with floc=0 expects strictly positive fitting values. Investigate zeros, negatives, missing cells, unit errors, and clock failures before calling fit.

Fixing the location at zero without a reason. The constraint matches this lesson’s definition of elapsed repair time. It can be wrong for shifted measurements. Document the real lower boundary and test the assumption.

Choosing a distribution only because its curve looks familiar. A gamma model is one candidate for these positive, skewed values. Compare fitted and observed cumulative behavior, especially near important cutoffs. When the mismatch matters, test other justified families or calculate probabilities directly from the empirical distribution—the observed values without a fitted curve.

Treating fit() as proof of a data-generating law. Maximum likelihood returns parameter estimates inside the family you requested. It does not prove that the family is correct or that future operations remain stable.

Ignoring censored work. A completed-repairs table omits jobs still open at the extraction time. Those durations are right-censored: you know they have lasted at least a certain time, but not their final time. Dropping them can make completion look faster. Use a survival-analysis method that represents censoring when real data has this structure.

Mixing hours and minutes. A scale estimate in hours and a cutoff in minutes produce a valid-looking but meaningless answer. Store units in column names, variable names, labels, and output text.

Reading p90 as a guarantee. A model percentile describes a distribution under assumptions. Monitor observed coverage after deployment and refit or replace the model when the process changes.

Recap: Let the Question Choose the Function

Start by writing the event you need. Use cdf(x) for the modeled share at or below x, sf(x) for the share above x, and cdf(upper) - cdf(lower) for a bounded interval. Use ppf(q) when you know the target cumulative probability and need its value cutoff.

In this executed example, the fitted gamma model estimated 65.0% of repairs within eight hours, 12.1% beyond twelve hours, and 33.4% between six and ten hours. Its fitted p90 was 12.64 hours. The unused rows exposed a meaningful limitation: only 86.2% fell below that p90, and the observed over-twelve-hour share was higher than the model estimate.

The durable lesson is therefore two-part. Translate the direction of the question into the correct SciPy function, then check the fitted answer against held-out observations that represent future cases. Correct probability code cannot repair a poor distribution assumption, but a visible empirical check can reveal where that assumption needs work.
