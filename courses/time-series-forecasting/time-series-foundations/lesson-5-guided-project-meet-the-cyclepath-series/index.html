Lesson 5 - Guided Project: Meet the Cyclepath Series

Welcome to the Guided Project#

Across this module you studied time series one piece at a time. You learned what makes them a separate topic (order, autocorrelation, and splitting by time). You learned the pandas DatetimeIndex with resampling and rolling windows. You learned the chronological split with naive baselines. Now you will put it all together on one series, the one you will model for the rest of the course. Cyclepath, the made-up city bike-share, will go from a line of generator code to a fully explored series with a fair test set and a baseline that every future model has to beat. By the end, you will have built the series, seen its trend and yearly summer peaks, split off the last year without mixing it into training, and measured exactly how well the simplest reasonable forecast does. That number is the target for ARIMA, SARIMA, and everything else.

By the end of this project, you will be able to:

  • Generate the canonical Cyclepath monthly series with a DatetimeIndex and confirm its shape
  • Explore trend and seasonality with summary stats, resampling to yearly totals, and a rolling mean
  • Split the series chronologically, holding out the last 12 months as a leak-free test set
  • Establish naive and seasonal-naive baselines with MAE, RMSE, and MAPE, the target every model must beat

We’ll build it in stages, reusing the exact tools from each lesson in the module.

Let’s meet Cyclepath.

Stage 1: Generate the Series#

Start with the data itself. Cyclepath is seeded synthetic (generated in code with a fixed random seed), so every number you compute below matches the lesson exactly and you can rerun the whole analysis end to end. The generator builds a monthly DatetimeIndex (Lesson 2), adds a steady upward trend, a yearly seasonal wave that peaks in summer, and a little noise, then rounds to whole trips.

That freq="MS" means month start: one point stamped at the first of each month. You get exactly 96 monthly points spanning 2016-01 through 2023-12 (eight full years), and the first three values are [5907, 5955, 7843], the winter trough climbing toward spring. This is the series. Everything else in this project, and in this course, operates on y.

Stage 2: Explore It#

Before you split or model anything, look at what you have (Lesson 3). Start with the one-line summary:

Ridership ranges from a low of 5,907 to a high of 20,533, averaging about 13,267 trips a month. A line plot of y would show two things at once: a steady upward trend (the city adopting bikes as the network grows) and a yearly rhythm of tall summer peaks and deep winter troughs that repeats eight times. That is exactly the trend-plus-seasonality structure Lesson 1 promised.

To see the growth on its own, resample the monthly series to yearly totals. This collapses the seasonal wiggle so only the trend remains:

Annual trips climb every single year: 113,347 → 127,335 → 140,950 → 153,225 → 165,816 → 177,929 → 191,528 → 203,501 (2016 through 2023), roughly 80% growth over eight years, with no reversals. To smooth the monthly series without collapsing it, overlay a 12-month rolling mean (Lesson 2), which averages each month with the eleven before it, cancelling the seasonal cycle and leaving the trend:

The first 11 values are NaN (a 12-month window needs 12 points), and from there the rolling mean rises smoothly from about 9,446 to 16,958: the trend line under the seasonal noise, almost doubling as Cyclepath grows.

Stage 3: Split Chronologically#

Now the step that prevents you from fooling yourself (Lesson 4). To measure a forecast fairly, you need a test set the model has never seen. For a time series, that test set must come after the training data in time, never a random sample. You hold out the last 12 months (all of 2023) as the test set and train on everything before it:

That gives 84 training months (through 2022-12) and 12 test months (all of 2023). Notice there is no shuffle: the split is a single clean cut in time, so nothing from 2023 leaks backward into training. This mimics reality exactly: you stand at the end of 2022 and forecast the year you haven’t lived yet.

Stage 4: Baseline It#

A test set is only useful next to a baseline, the simplest reasonable forecast. The baseline tells you whether a more complex model is worth its extra effort (Lesson 4). Two baselines matter here. Naive forecasts every future month as the last value observed. Seasonal-naive forecasts each month as the value from 12 months earlier. It replays last year’s shape, which fits a seasonal series well.

The plain naive forecast is a flat line at December 2022’s value. It ignores seasonality entirely and is very wrong for most of the year: MAE 3,497 trips, MAPE 19.0%. The seasonal-naive forecast replays 2022’s monthly pattern and tracks 2023 closely: MAE 998 trips, MAPE 5.9%. This is more than three times better, because Cyclepath is seasonal and last year’s shape is a strong guess for this year’s. That 5.9% MAPE is the number every later model must beat. Any model you build for the rest of the course has to score below it, or it is not worth its complexity.

Every later model is measured against the baseline

Seasonal-naive at 5.9% MAPE is the score every future model must beat. A model that cannot beat this simple “same month last year” forecast has learned nothing worth the added complexity, tuning, and risk, no matter how advanced it looks. This is a core discipline of forecasting: always build a naive baseline first, then make each new model prove it is better. If your ARIMA cannot beat replaying last year, use the baseline instead.

Stage 5: The Takeaway#

Step back and look at what this project produced. You now hold three things you’ll use for the entire rest of the course:

  1. The Cyclepath series: 96 months of seeded, reproducible monthly ridership with a clear upward trend and strong yearly seasonality, built from one cyclepath() function.
  2. A leak-free chronological test set: the last 12 months (2023) held out with no shuffle, so any accuracy you report is the accuracy you would get standing at the end of 2022.
  3. A baseline to beat: seasonal-naive at 5.9% MAPE, the target every future model is judged against.

That is the whole point of Module 1: not a model yet, but the scaffolding every future model needs to be trustworthy, namely a fixed series, a fair test set, and a baseline. From here, the work is beating that baseline. Next up, Module 2 takes this exact series and decomposes it, separating the trend, the seasonality, and the leftover residual into their own components, so you can see and eventually model each piece on its own.

Practice Exercises#

Exercise 1: Change the horizon to 24 months#

Instead of holding out the last 12 months, hold out the last 24 (all of 2022 and 2023).

  • Re-split with h = 24 and train, test = y.iloc[:-h], y.iloc[-h:], and note the new train and test lengths.
  • Recompute the seasonal-naive baseline for the 24-month horizon by tiling the last 12 training months twice.
  • Compare its MAPE with the 5.9% from the 12-month horizon.
  • Explain why a longer test period is usually harder to forecast.

Hint

Set h = 24 and re-run the split with train, test = y.iloc[:-h], y.iloc[-h:]. This gives 72 training months and 24 test months. The standard seasonal-naive forecast repeats the last observed season for every future year. So you predict both 2022 and 2023 using 2021, by tiling the last 12 training months twice: pd.Series(np.tile(train.iloc[-12:].values, 2), index=test.index). This scores a MAPE of 10.1%. Compare it to the 5.9% MAPE from the 12-month horizon. A longer horizon is usually harder, because you forecast further from the data you have, and the trend keeps rising, so repeating an older year falls further behind a growing series. (An alternative is the lag-24 forecast, pd.Series(train.iloc[-24:].values, index=test.index), which predicts each month from exactly 24 months earlier. On this series it scores a worse 13.2% MAPE, because 2020 and 2021 are further in the past than the last observed season.)

Exercise 2: Compute a rolling standard deviation#

You overlaid a 12-month rolling mean to see the trend. Now look at the rolling spread.

  • Compute a 12-month rolling standard deviation with y.rolling(12).std().
  • Describe in plain terms what that line measures.
  • Say whether the seasonal swing looks constant across the years or growing, and what each case would imply for modeling.

Hint

The rolling std measures how much the series varies within each 12-month window — essentially the amplitude of the seasonal swing plus noise. If it grows over time, the summer-to-winter gap is widening as ridership climbs, which is a sign of multiplicative seasonality (the swing scales with the level) rather than additive. That distinction is exactly what Module 2 formalizes when it asks whether to model the series as trend-plus-seasonality or trend-times-seasonality.

Exercise 3: Why lag 12, not lag 1?#

Seasonal-naive forecasts each month using the value from 12 months earlier, not the value from 1 month earlier.

  • Explain why lag 12 is the right choice for Cyclepath’s monthly series.
  • Say what forecast you get if you use lag 1 instead, and roughly how it scores.
  • State the lag you would use for daily data with a weekly cycle.

Hint

The lag should match the length of the seasonal cycle. Cyclepath repeats every 12 months (each July resembles last July far more than it resembles this June), so lag 12 replays the matching month. Lag 1 would just repeat the previous month and drift with the seasonal wave, which is the plain naive forecast that scored 19.0%. For daily data with a weekly cycle, the season length is 7, so you would forecast each day as the value from 7 days earlier (last Monday predicts this Monday).

Summary#

You built and explored the Cyclepath series end to end and set the baseline the rest of the course must beat. You generated the series with cyclepath(): 96 monthly points on a DatetimeIndex from 2016-01 to 2023-12, starting [5907, 5955, 7843]. You explored it: min 5,907, mean about 13,267, max 20,533; a line plot revealing upward trend plus yearly summer peaks; yearly totals climbing 113,347 → 203,501; and a 12-month rolling mean rising from about 9,446 to 16,958. You split it chronologically: 84 training months through 2022-12, 12 test months across 2023, no shuffle. And you baselined it: naive scored MAE 3,497 / MAPE 19.0%, while seasonal-naive scored MAE 998 / MAPE 5.9%, all computed with pandas and numpy. Seasonal-naive at 5.9% is the target every later model must beat. You now have the series, a leak-free test set, and a baseline to beat.

Key Concepts#

  • Seeded synthetic series — Cyclepath is generated with a fixed seed, so every number is reproducible and matches exactly on rerun.
  • Explore before you model — summary stats, a line plot, yearly resampling, and a rolling mean reveal the trend and seasonality you’ll exploit.
  • Chronological hold-out — cut off the last 12 months as a test set with no shuffle, so reported accuracy reflects real forecasting.
  • Naive baselines as the target — seasonal-naive (lag 12) at 5.9% MAPE is the score every future model must beat to justify its complexity.

Why This Matters#

This project is the foundation the whole course rests on. Without a fixed series, a fair test set, and a baseline, every model you build afterward would be measured against nothing. A model measured against nothing can fool you into using something worse than replaying last year. By setting seasonal-naive at 5.9% MAPE now, you have given every future technique a clear target and a fair scoreboard. This includes decomposition, ARIMA, SARIMA, and backtesting. Once this foundation is in place, the rest of the course keeps asking one question: did this model beat the baseline? Next, Module 2 decomposes Cyclepath into its trend, seasonality, and residual so you can model each piece on its own.

Continue Building Your Skills#

You now have the Cyclepath series, a clean chronological test set, and a baseline to beat (seasonal-naive at 5.9% MAPE), which is exactly the scaffolding every serious forecasting project starts from. With the foundation in place, the next module stops treating the series as one signal and pulls it apart: isolating the trend, the yearly seasonality, and the leftover residual so each can be understood and modeled on its own.
