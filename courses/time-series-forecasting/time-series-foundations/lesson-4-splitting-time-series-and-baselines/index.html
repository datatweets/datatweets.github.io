Lesson 4 - Splitting Time Series and Baselines

Welcome to Splitting Time Series and Baselines#

Before you build any model, you need two things. First, a fair way to test a forecast. Second, a simple forecast to compare against. In Lesson 1 you saw why a random split fails: it mixes future data into training. In this lesson you will split the data by time instead. You will keep the most recent months hidden, as a test set. Then you will build two “baselines”. These are forecasts so simple they take one line of code each. Their job is not to be good. Their job is to set the bar. If the ARIMA model you build in a later module cannot beat a one-line seasonal-naive guess, then the extra complexity is not worth it. You want to know that before you use the model. This lesson gives you the fair split and the baselines every model must beat.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Split a time series chronologically into train and test, holding out the last H months
  • Define the forecast horizon and explain why the test set must come after the train set
  • Build naive and seasonal-naive baseline forecasts in a line of code each
  • Compute MAE, RMSE, and MAPE, and read seasonal-naive as the bar a real model must beat

Let’s start with the split that does not mix the future into training.

The Chronological Split and the Horizon#

You forecast the future from the past. So your test set must come after your training data in time. You do not shuffle, and you do not use train_test_split(random_state=…). Instead, you slice off the last H observations as the test set. You keep everything before them for training. H is the forecast horizon, which is how many steps ahead you commit to predicting. For Cyclepath, you hold out the last 12 months, a full year, so the test set covers every season.

This prints 84 2022-12-01 and 12 2023-01-01 2023-12-01. There are 84 months of training that end in December 2022, and 12 months of test that run through all of 2023. Notice what is missing here. There is no shuffle and no random seed on the split. Reordering would mix 2023 into the training data and let the model see the future it is supposed to predict. That is the leaky split from Lesson 1. Slicing by position on a time-ordered index keeps the past and the future cleanly separated.

One split gives you one score, which can be lucky or unlucky. For a more reliable evaluation, you would slide the split forward again and again. You would train on a growing window, test the next H months, and average the results. This technique is called walk-forward validation. You will study it in full in a later module (Evaluation & Backtesting). For now, a single held-out year is enough to set up your baselines.

Two Baselines Every Model Must Beat#

A baseline is a very simple forecast, only one line of code. That simplicity is the point. If your advanced model cannot beat the baseline, then the extra work is not helping. Two baselines cover almost every series.

  • Naive forecasts every future month as the last observed value. Whatever ridership was in December 2022, you predict that exact number for all twelve months of 2023.
  • Seasonal-naive forecasts each month as the value from one full season earlier. With monthly data and yearly seasonality, this means “this coming July looks like last July”, month by month across the horizon.

Naive is a flat line. It holds December’s level all year and ignores that summers rise and winters fall. Seasonal-naive replays the previous year, so it rises and falls with the calendar exactly the way Cyclepath does. On a series with strong seasonality, this difference is large, and the metrics will show it.

Scoring Forecasts: MAE, RMSE, and MAPE#

A forecast needs a score. Three error metrics do almost all the work, and each one answers a slightly different question. In the formulas below, yt y_t is the actual value, y^t \hat{y}_t is the forecast, and n n is the number of forecast points.

MAE (mean absolute error) is the average size of the miss, in the data’s own units, which are trips here.

MAE=1n∑t=1n∣yt−y^t∣ \mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} \lvert y_t - \hat{y}_t \rvert

RMSE (root mean squared error) squares the errors before averaging, so it punishes large misses more than small ones.

RMSE=1n∑t=1n(yt−y^t)2 \mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} (y_t - \hat{y}_t)^2}

MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) writes the miss as a percentage of the actual value, so it has no units and is easy to explain.

MAPE=100n∑t=1n∣yt−y^tyt∣ \mathrm{MAPE} = \frac{100}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} \left\lvert \frac{y_t - \hat{y}_t}{y_t} \right\rvert

One warning about MAPE: it divides by the actual value yt y_t , so it breaks if any actual value is 0. Cyclepath is always well above 0, so this is not a problem here.

Read these numbers carefully, because they set the goal for the rest of the course. Seasonal-naive is about 3.5 times more accurate than plain naive. Its MAE is 998 versus 3,497, and its MAPE is 5.9% versus 19.0%. It earns this entirely by capturing the yearly pattern that the flat forecast throws away. This is now the bar. Any ARIMA or SARIMA model you build later must beat seasonal-naive’s 5.9% MAPE to be worth its added complexity. A model that cannot beat a one-line replay of last year is not adding value, no matter how advanced its equations look.

Always beat the baseline — or explain why you can’t

Compute the naive and seasonal-naive baselines before you fit anything. Write their MAPE at the top of your notebook. From then on, every model reports its own MAPE right next to that number. If your advanced model does not beat seasonal-naive, one of three things is true. Your series has weak seasonality. Or your model is set up wrong. Or the extra complexity is genuinely not helping, in which case you should just use the one-line baseline. The baseline is not a check you pass once. It is the measuring stick you hold up against every result for the rest of the project.

Practice Exercises#

Exercise 1: Why not just shuffle and split 80/20?#

A teammate wants to run train_test_split(y, test_size=0.2, random_state=0) on the Cyclepath series “like we always do.” Why is that wrong here, and what do you do instead?

Hint

A random 80/20 split scatters future months (2023) into the training set and past months into the test set, so the model trains on data from after the period it’s scored on — it sees the future, and the test score is meaningless. Forecasting requires a chronological split: sort by time and hold out the last H observations (y.iloc[:-h], y.iloc[-h:]). Train on the earliest months, test on the latest, mirroring how a real forecast runs — you never have tomorrow’s data when you predict tomorrow.

Exercise 2: Why is seasonal-naive so much better than naive here?#

Naive scores 19.0% MAPE and seasonal-naive scores 5.9% on the same held-out year. What property of Cyclepath makes the gap so large, and when would the gap get smaller?

Hint

Cyclepath has strong yearly seasonality, with big summer peaks and deep winter lows. So a flat “last value” forecast is badly wrong for most of the year, while “same month last year” lands close. The gap is large because the seasonality is strong. On a series with little or no seasonality, such as a slow random walk, naive and seasonal-naive would score about the same. Plain naive might even win, because it uses the most recent value rather than one from a year ago.

Exercise 3: Reading MAE against RMSE#

For seasonal-naive, MAE is 998 and RMSE is 1,024 — very close. For naive, MAE is 3,497 and RMSE is 4,219 — RMSE noticeably larger. What does the size of the RMSE-minus-MAE gap tell you?

Hint

RMSE squares errors before averaging, so a few large misses pull it up. When RMSE sits well above MAE, the errors are uneven, and some months are badly wrong. Naive’s wider gap (4,219 versus 3,497) reflects its huge misses at the seasonal peaks and lows. Seasonal-naive’s tight gap (1,024 versus 998) shows that its errors are small and steady across the year. Report MAE for the typical miss, RMSE when big misses are especially costly, and MAPE to share the error as a plain percentage.

Summary#

Forecasting is tested with a chronological split, never a random one. You hold out the last H observations, the forecast horizon, as a test set that comes after your training data. This scores the model the way it will actually run. For Cyclepath, you kept 84 months of training through December 2022 and tested on all of 2023. Then you built two baselines: naive (predict the last observed value) and seasonal-naive (predict the value from one season earlier). Scored with MAE (average miss in units), RMSE (which punishes big misses), and MAPE (a unit-free percentage), seasonal-naive beat naive by about 3.5 times, with a MAPE of 5.9% versus 19.0%. It wins because it captures the yearly pattern that naive ignores. That 5.9% is now the bar: any model you build later must beat it to justify its complexity.

Key Concepts#

  • Chronological split — slice off the last H observations; test must be later than train, never shuffled.
  • Forecast horizon (H) — how many steps ahead you predict; here, 12 months.
  • Naive & seasonal-naive — one-line baselines: last value, and same period last season.
  • MAE / RMSE / MAPE — typical miss in units, squared-penalty on big misses, and unit-free percentage.

Why This Matters#

Most forecasting failures fall into two kinds. The first is a testing failure: a leaky split makes a weak model look excellent. The second is a value failure: a complex model never actually beat a one-line guess. A chronological split and a pair of baselines close both gaps in a few lines of code. You get a score you can trust and a bar that every future model must beat. This is the discipline that keeps the rest of the course grounded. When you fit SARIMA and read its MAPE, you will know at once whether it earned its place by comparing it to the 5.9% you set today. Next, you will bring all four foundation lessons together on the full Cyclepath series in a hands-on guided project.
