Welcome to The Datetime Index and Resampling#

In Lesson 1 you learned why a time series is a different kind of thing. Now you will learn how to store and work with one in pandas. These are the everyday tools you will use in every lesson that follows. The key idea is that pandas does not treat dates as plain labels. When a Series is indexed by a DatetimeIndex, pandas understands the calendar. It knows that January follows December, that twelve months make a year, and that a “quarter” and a “week” are real spans of time. This calendar awareness lets you do three things. You can resample a series to a new frequency. You can roll a window across it. You can line up two series on real dates. None of this would work if your dates were just text strings. In this lesson you will build the Cyclepath series, inspect its index, and use the two main operations, resampling and rolling.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Build and inspect a DatetimeIndex and read its frequency
  • Reproduce the Cyclepath series that runs through the whole course
  • Resample a series to a coarser frequency with an aggregation like .sum()
  • Smooth a series with a rolling window and explain what a 12-month mean reveals

Let’s start with the index that makes all of this possible.

The DatetimeIndex: Why the Index Matters#

In pandas, a time series is a Series (or DataFrame) whose index is a DatetimeIndex. This is an ordered set of timestamps instead of plain integer positions. The index carries a frequency, which is a code that says how the timestamps are spaced. "MS" means month start, "YS" means year start, and "D" means daily. Because the index holds real calendar dates and pandas knows their spacing, it can do things a plain integer index cannot. It can add up every point in a year, average the last twelve months, or line up two series that share dates. Everything in this lesson, and most of this course, depends on this.

You build a datetime index most often with pd.date_range, which creates evenly spaced timestamps at a given frequency. Below is the canonical Cyclepath generator, the exact code that defines the series you will model in every lesson. Copy it exactly. The fixed seed means your numbers will match the lesson to the digit.

Read that output slowly. y.index.freqstr is MS, so pandas knows these are month-start timestamps, one per month. There are 96 of them, running from 2016-01-01 to 2023-12-01. That is exactly eight years of monthly data, from January 2016 through December 2023. The first three trip counts are [5907, 5955, 7843]. These are low winter months that are already climbing toward spring. That single Series, index and values together, is Cyclepath.

It helps to see the formula behind the generator. Each monthly value yt y_t is the sum of three parts: a straight-line trend, a repeating seasonal wave, and random noise εt \varepsilon_t . Here t t is the month number, from 0 to 95.

yt=9000+90 t⏟trend+3200sin⁡ ⁣(2π(t−3)/12)⏟seasonality+εt⏟noise y_t = \underbrace{9000 + 90\,t}_{\text{trend}} + \underbrace{3200 \sin\!\big(2\pi (t-3)/12\big)}_{\text{seasonality}} + \underbrace{\varepsilon_t}_{\text{noise}}

The trend part, 9000+90 t 9000 + 90\,t , starts at 9000 and rises by 90 trips each month. The seasonal part is a sine wave with a period of 12 months, so it repeats every year. The noise εt \varepsilon_t is drawn from a normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 350. This “trend plus seasonality plus noise” structure is exactly what the next module will learn to pull apart.

Frequency strings you’ll see all course

Pandas names frequencies with short codes. The current codes are: D daily, W weekly, MS month start, ME month end, QS quarter start, QE quarter end, YS year start, and YE year end. The S suffix means the timestamp lands on the start of the period. The E suffix means it lands on the end. Cyclepath uses MS, so each month is stamped on the 1st. Older code sometimes uses the single letters M, Q, and Y for month end, quarter end, and year end. Those older forms still appear in tutorials but are deprecated in pandas 2.2 and later, so prefer ME, QE, and YE. The frequency lives on the index (y.index.freqstr). It is what lets pandas resample and roll correctly. Without it, pandas would only see a pile of dates and would not know that twelve of them make a year.

Resampling: Change the Frequency#

Resampling changes the frequency of a series. You regroup the timestamps into a coarser or finer calendar bucket. Going to a coarser frequency, such as monthly to yearly, is downsampling. Because you are collapsing many points into one, you must tell pandas how to combine them. You do this with an aggregation like .sum() or .mean(). Going to a finer frequency, such as monthly to daily, is upsampling. Upsampling creates gaps that you have to fill or interpolate. This lesson focuses on downsampling with a sum. You will add the twelve monthly trip counts in each year into one yearly total.

resample("YS") groups the index into year-start buckets. .sum() adds the twelve monthly values in each bucket into a single number. Notice that pandas handled the calendar work for you. It knew which months belong to 2016 and which to 2017, and it stamped each total on January 1st of its year. Now read down the column: 113347, 127335, 140950, …, 203501. Every year is larger than the one before it. The series climbs steadily from about 113,000 trips to over 203,000. This steady year-over-year growth is the trend you met in Lesson 1. Summing away the within-year changes makes the trend easy to see. Resampling to a coarser frequency is one of the fastest ways to see a trend.

Rolling Windows: Smooth at the Same Frequency#

A rolling window is different from resampling. It keeps the original frequency but slides a fixed-width window across the series. For each window, it computes a statistic. .rolling(12).mean() takes every trailing 12-month span and averages it. This produces a smoothed series at the same monthly frequency, with one output point per input point, once there is enough history.

There are two things to explain here. First, the first 11 values are NaN. A 12-month window needs 12 points to fill. So the earliest month with a full window is index 11, the 12th point, where the rolling mean first becomes valid at 9446. That is why ma.iloc[11] is the first real number and ma.dropna().iloc[0] is the same 9446. Second, and this is the main point, a 12-month rolling mean averages exactly one full seasonal cycle. The summer peaks and winter lows cancel each other out. What remains is the trend on its own. The smoothed series rises from about 9,446 early on to about 16,958 at the end. The seasonality is gone, and only the slow upward movement is left. Choosing a window equal to your seasonal period is the standard way to isolate the trend.

Practice Exercises#

Exercise 1: Read the frequency#

You load a series and y.index.freqstr returns MS, while len(y) is 96 and the last index is 2023-12-01. Without printing anything else, how many years of data do you have and how are the months stamped?

Hint

MS is month start, so every timestamp lands on the 1st of its month. 96 monthly points is 96 ÷ 12 = 8 years, and since the last point is December 2023, the series runs January 2016 through December 2023. Reading the frequency and length tells you the span and spacing before you plot a single point — that’s exactly what the index is for.

Exercise 2: Downsample or smooth?#

You want a single number for total trips in each calendar year. Do you reach for .resample(...) or .rolling(...), and what aggregation do you use?

Hint

Use y.resample("YS").sum(). You’re changing the frequency — collapsing twelve monthly points into one yearly bucket — which is downsampling, and it needs an aggregation to combine the points; a total means .sum(). .rolling(...) would keep the monthly frequency and just smooth it, giving you one value per month, not one per year. Resample when you want fewer, coarser points; roll when you want the same points, smoothed.

Exercise 3: Why 12, and why the NaNs?#

You run y.rolling(12).mean() and notice the first 11 values are NaN, and that the summer bumps have vanished from the smoothed line. Explain both.

Hint

The NaNs appear because a 12-wide window can’t be filled until 12 points exist, so the first valid value is at index 11 (the 12th month). The seasonality vanishes because a 12-month window spans exactly one full yearly cycle: averaging a complete cycle cancels the peaks against the troughs, leaving only the trend. That’s why a window equal to the seasonal period is the go-to choice for isolating trend from a monthly series.

Summary#

In pandas, a time series is a Series indexed by a DatetimeIndex. That index carries a frequency (Cyclepath’s is MS, month start) that makes pandas calendar-aware. This is the reason resampling, rolling, and alignment work at all. You reproduced the canonical Cyclepath generator: 96 monthly points from January 2016 to December 2023, seeded so every number matches the lesson. Resampling changes the frequency. y.resample("YS").sum() downsampled the monthly series into yearly totals, from 113,347 up to 203,501, which made the year-over-year trend easy to see. Downsampling always needs an aggregation. Rolling windows keep the frequency but slide a fixed span across the series. y.rolling(12).mean() left the first 11 months NaN, because a 12-wide window needs 12 points. By averaging one full seasonal cycle, it canceled the seasonality and revealed the trend rising from about 9,446 to 16,958.

Key Concepts#

  • DatetimeIndex — an index of real timestamps that carries a frequency and makes pandas understand the calendar.
  • Frequency strings — short codes like MS, YS, D that say how the timestamps are spaced.
  • Resampling — changing the frequency; downsampling to a coarser bucket needs an aggregation like .sum().
  • Rolling window — a sliding fixed-width window at the same frequency; a period-length mean cancels seasonality and shows trend.

Why This Matters#

Almost every time-series operation you will write assumes your data sits on a proper DatetimeIndex with a known frequency. This includes decomposition, differencing, backtesting, and forecasting. When the index is wrong, these operations misbehave in quiet ways. Resampling and rolling are two tools you will use constantly. You resample to change the level of detail in a question, such as “give me yearly totals”. You roll to smooth away noise or seasonality and show the signal underneath. Getting comfortable with them now pays off later. When you decompose Cyclepath in the next module, or compare a forecast against real values, the mechanics will feel natural and you can focus on the modeling. Next, you will use these tools to explore and visualize the series and read its structure by eye.

Continue Building Your Skills#

You can now work with a real time series in pandas. You can build and inspect a DatetimeIndex, read its frequency, resample it to a coarser frequency with an aggregation, and smooth it with a rolling window that cancels seasonality and reveals the trend. These are the everyday tools behind every technique to come. Next you will use them visually. You will plot Cyclepath, view it season by season, and learn to read trend, seasonality, and noise straight off a chart before you build a single model.
