Welcome to the Pilot Scorecard in Practice#

Day 11 gave you the bounded pilot charter and introduced the shape of a scorecard: five KPI categories, offline and online evidence, and a three-outcome decision gate. That’s the structure. What it didn’t cover is the part that actually trips up most pilots — measuring human experience as more than a guess, and knowing whether the evidence you’ve collected is actually enough to trust a threshold you’re about to act on.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Measure adoption and human experience with real instruments, not impressions
  • Judge whether a pilot’s sample size and duration are enough to trust its evidence
  • Fill in a complete scorecard across all six real dimensions with actual numbers
  • Finalize a Pilot Scorecard and Decision Gate ready for a real go/no-go conversation

Measuring Human Experience Honestly#

“People seem to like it” is not a measurement. The frontline users from Day 10’s role map are the people whose actual experience determines whether a pilot that looks good on paper survives contact with daily use. Measure it with real instruments:

  • Override rate. How often does a person reject or change the agent’s suggestion? A rising override rate over the pilot’s duration is an early warning a pass rate alone won’t show — even if accuracy looks stable, trust may be eroding.
  • Time-on-task, not just outcome. An agent that produces the “right” category but takes a person longer to verify than they’d have spent deciding it themselves hasn’t actually saved time — it’s moved the work, not removed it.
  • A short, structured survey. Three to five questions, asked on a fixed cadence (weekly, not just at the end): does this help you do your job faster, do you trust its suggestions, what’s the most common thing you have to fix. Open-ended feedback matters, but a fixed set of questions asked consistently is what lets you compare week to week.
  • Workaround behavior. Are people quietly going around the agent — reverting to the old process for cases they don’t trust it on? This is often the most honest signal of all, and the one a dashboard full of accuracy numbers won’t surface on its own.

A pilot with a strong pass rate and a rising override rate, longer time-on-task, and quiet workarounds is not a pilot that’s working — it’s a pilot that looks fine in the metrics engineering tracks and is failing in the metric that actually decides whether it survives rollout.

How Much Evidence Is Enough?#

Pilots run for a bounded time on a bounded population, which means every number you collect has real uncertainty behind it — a question Day 11’s charter doesn’t dig into.

  • Small samples produce noisy rates. Ten cases with nine correct is 90% — and also consistent with a true rate anywhere from roughly 60% to 99%. A hundred cases with ninety correct is a genuinely tighter estimate of the same 90%. Don’t treat a small early sample as settled evidence either direction.
  • Duration matters as much as volume. A workflow with seasonal or weekly patterns (support volume spikes on Mondays, expense reports cluster at month-end) needs a pilot long enough to see more than one cycle, or the evidence is systematically incomplete.
  • State your confidence honestly in the scorecard. “82% pass rate, n=47, two weeks” is more useful to a decision-maker than “82% pass rate” alone — the first number tells them how much to trust the second.
  • A pilot that’s too small to trust either way is itself a finding. The honest recommendation may be “extend the pilot” rather than continue or stop — a legitimate, explicit third option, not a failure to decide.

Worked Example: Cascade Analytics’ Completed Scorecard#

The following is a hypothetical, composite example for teaching purposes — not a real company or a documented case study.

Two weeks into the support-triage pilot, n=1,840 tickets (roughly 130/day, both weekdays and one full weekend cycle):

  • Business outcome: Baseline 40 min average routing time → measured 14 min (tickets where the agent’s suggestion was accepted). Continue threshold ≤20 min: met.
  • Task quality: Baseline n/a → 84% of agent suggestions accepted without correction, n=1,840. Continue threshold ≥75%: met.
  • Safety: Baseline 8% mis-routing (historical) → 6% mis-routing on accepted suggestions, no billing-adjacent tickets mis-routed (the one hard safety line from Day 7’s threat register). Continue threshold: no billing-adjacent mis-routes, and ≤8% overall: met.
  • Adoption (override rate): Week 1: 19% override. Week 2: 22% override — a small rise worth watching, not yet a red flag. Continue threshold <30%, watch trend: met, flagged for re-check.
  • Human experience (survey): Weekly 4-question survey, 5 of 6 triage staff responding: “saves me time” averaged 4.1/5 in week 1, 3.8/5 in week 2 — consistent with the small override-rate rise. Continue threshold ≥3.5/5: met, same trend flagged.
  • Latency/cost: Suggestions return in under 2 seconds; monthly cost tracking $420, within the $400–600 estimate from Day 4. Continue threshold ≤$600/month: met.

Roll-up recommendation: Continue, with one named change — investigate the week-1-to-week-2 override and satisfaction trend before extending the pilot to full volume; if it’s a specific, fixable pattern (a ticket category the agent handles inconsistently), fix it and re-measure for one more week before declaring the pilot fully successful. This is a Change, not a Stop — the evidence is still favorable overall, but the honest scorecard surfaced a real trend a single end-of-pilot snapshot would have missed entirely.

Exercise: Complete Your Own Scorecard#

Using Day 11’s charter and thresholds, design the override-rate and survey instruments you’d actually use, and fill in the scorecard table with realistic placeholder numbers for a two-week pilot. Write a roll-up recommendation that names Continue, Change, or Stop per row, not just as one overall verdict — and if any row’s sample size is too small to trust, say so.

Common Failure Modes#

  • Trusting “people seem happy” over a real instrument. Impressions drift; a fixed survey asked on a fixed cadence doesn’t.
  • Treating a small early sample as settled. Ten cases is a starting point, not evidence strong enough to act on either direction.
  • Averaging away a real problem. A single overall score can hide one dimension that’s actually failing — report per-dimension status before rolling up.
  • No trend visibility. A single end-of-pilot snapshot misses a metric that’s quietly moving in the wrong direction, as Cascade’s override rate shows.

Summary#

A trustworthy pilot scorecard measures human experience with real instruments — override rate, time-on-task, a consistent survey, workaround behavior — not impressions. It states how much confidence a sample size and duration actually support, rather than treating any number as settled. And it reports status per KPI dimension before rolling up to one recommendation, so a real problem in one dimension can’t hide behind good news in another.

Key Concepts#

  • Real human-experience instruments — override rate, time-on-task, structured survey, workaround behavior.
  • Evidence confidence — sample size and duration change how much a number should be trusted.
  • Per-dimension status — Continue, Change, or Stop named for each KPI before rolling up.

Why This Matters#

The decision gate is where a pilot’s evidence meets a real go/no-go conversation with real consequences. A scorecard that measures human experience honestly and reports confidence explicitly is the difference between a decision grounded in evidence and one grounded in whichever metric happened to look best in the summary slide.
