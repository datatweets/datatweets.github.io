Welcome to Misinformation and Incident Response#

Grounding an agent’s answers in real, retrieved sources — the technical work covered elsewhere in this course — measurably reduces fabrication. It does not make it zero. Every organization running an agent in production eventually has a moment where it produced something fabricated, manipulated, stale, or simply ungrounded, and that content reached a customer, an employee, or another system. The question isn’t whether this happens; it’s whether anyone knows what to do in the first hour.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify where fabricated, manipulated, stale, or ungrounded content could affect customers, staff, decisions, or downstream systems
  • Define detection, escalation, correction, containment, and evidence-retention steps before you need them
  • Plan the communication a real incident requires — internally and, where relevant, externally
  • Produce an AI Incident Mini-Playbook scoped to your specific pilot

Four Ways Content Goes Wrong#

Each has a different cause and a different first response:

  • Fabricated. The agent generated something with no basis in any real source — a policy that doesn’t exist, a number it made up, a citation to a document that isn’t there.
  • Manipulated. The content was correct at the source but altered somewhere in the pipeline — a prompt-injection attempt (Day 7) that succeeded, or a bug that corrupted retrieved text before it reached the output.
  • Stale. The content was accurate when the underlying document was written, but the document has since changed and the agent retrieved an outdated version.
  • Ungrounded. The agent answered a question its sources don’t actually address, without saying so — the grounding gate covered in the more technical lesson exists specifically to catch this case before it reaches a person.

Knowing which of the four you’re dealing with changes the response: a fabrication needs a correction and a look at why grounding didn’t catch it; staleness needs a content-freshness fix, not a security investigation; a manipulation needs Day 7’s threat register reopened.

Where It Reaches#

The damage from bad content depends entirely on where it lands. Map this before an incident, not during one:

  • Customers. A wrong answer in a support interaction, a fabricated policy stated as fact, an incorrect price or term.
  • Staff. An internal decision made on a fabricated summary or a stale policy document the agent surfaced as current.
  • Downstream systems. Content that flows automatically into another system — a report, a dashboard, another agent’s input — without a person reading it first, which is where a single bad output can compound.
  • Decisions. Anywhere the content directly informs a choice with real consequences, not just information someone reads and discards.

The highest-severity incidents are the ones that reach a downstream system or a consequential decision before any person sees the content — exactly the case Day 6’s authority mapping (recommend vs. execute) and Day 7’s monitoring controls exist to slow down.

The Five Steps of a Real Incident Response#

  1. Detection. How would anyone actually notice — a user report, an automated monitoring alert (Day 7), a routine review catching it, or nothing until it’s escalated externally? A plan with no detection mechanism relies on luck.
  2. Escalation. Who gets notified, how fast, and what’s the trigger? A customer complaint about one wrong answer and a fabricated statement already live on a public-facing page warrant very different urgency.
  3. Containment. What stops the bleeding — pausing the agent for this workflow, reverting a specific output, restricting authority back to a lower level from Day 6’s scale until the cause is understood?
  4. Correction. Fixing the specific wrong content wherever it landed — a customer follow-up, a corrected internal document, a downstream system re-run with the right input.
  5. Evidence retention. Keeping the logs (from Day 6’s integration map), the exact prompt and output, and the timeline — both to diagnose the cause and because a real incident may need to be reconstructed later for a stakeholder, a regulator, or a legal review.

Communication Is Part of the Response, Not an Afterthought#

Who needs to know, and what do they need to be told? This is a deliberate plan, not an improvised one written during the incident itself:

  • Internal. The team running the pilot, their leadership, and anyone whose workflow the incident affected — told promptly and accurately, including what’s not yet known.
  • External. If a customer or the public was affected, what’s said, by whom, and approved by whom before it goes out — this is where legal and communications functions, not just engineering, need a defined role in advance.
  • Honesty about uncertainty. Early communication that overstates what’s known (“this was an isolated incident”) before an investigation confirms it is itself a form of the same problem — say what you know, what you don’t yet know, and when you’ll know more.

Worked Example: Cascade Analytics’ Mini-Playbook#

The following is a hypothetical, composite example for teaching purposes — not a real company or a documented case study.

For the support-triage agent: Detection — the weekly sampling review (from Day 7’s monitoring control) plus any triage staff member can flag a suspicious categorization directly, any time. Escalation — a single mis-categorization goes to the customer-success lead within the day; a pattern of three or more similar mis-categorizations in a week escalates to the pilot’s engineering and security contacts within the hour. Containment — the pilot’s authority can be dropped from Recommend back to Read-only (agent still runs, suggestions no longer shown) by the engineering lead alone, without waiting for a wider review, if the pattern suggests a systematic problem. Correction — mis-routed tickets are manually re-routed by the triage team as part of normal work; no downstream system consumes the categorization automatically, so no re-run is needed at this stage. Evidence retention — the ticket text, the agent’s suggested category and confidence score, and the staff member’s actual routing decision are retained for 90 days, matching the team’s existing ticket-retention policy. Internal communication — the customer-success lead and the pilot’s engineering owner, within one business day of any escalation-triggering event. External communication — not applicable at this stage, since the agent only recommends and a person still decides before anything reaches a customer; this section is revisited if authority is ever raised.

Exercise: Build Your Own Mini-Playbook#

Using your Day 6 integration and authority map and Day 7 threat register, fill in the Incident Mini-Playbook above. Be specific about who — a role, not “the team” — for escalation, containment authority, and communication approval.

Common Failure Modes#

  • No detection mechanism, relying on luck. If nobody would notice until a customer escalates publicly, that’s the actual detection plan — worth stating honestly and improving before launch, not after.
  • Containment with no named owner. “The agent gets paused” — by whom, and do they know they have that authority?
  • Correction that stops at the direct interaction. If the content reached a downstream system, the correction has to reach that system too, not just the original conversation.
  • No evidence-retention plan. Reconstructing what happened without logs turns every incident review into guesswork.
  • Communication improvised during the incident. Deciding who approves an external message while the incident is happening adds delay exactly when speed matters most.

Summary#

Content can go wrong four distinct ways — fabricated, manipulated, stale, or ungrounded — and each reaches customers, staff, downstream systems, or decisions differently, with different consequences. A real incident response names detection, escalation, containment, correction, and evidence retention concretely, with owners, not generically. Communication — internal and external — is planned in advance, not improvised during the incident, and includes being honest about what isn’t yet known.

Key Concepts#

  • Four failure types — fabricated, manipulated, stale, ungrounded — each with a different cause and first response.
  • Five response steps — detection, escalation, containment, correction, evidence retention.
  • Communication plan — internal and external, with named approvers, decided in advance.

Why This Matters#

Grounding reduces how often this happens; it does not make the incident plan optional. An organization that can say “here’s exactly what we do in the first hour” turns an inevitable bad output into a contained, documented, correctable event — instead of the first time anyone thought about it being the moment it’s already happening publicly.
