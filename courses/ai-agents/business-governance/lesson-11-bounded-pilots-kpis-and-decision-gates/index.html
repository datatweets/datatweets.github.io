Welcome to Bounded Pilots, KPIs, and Decision Gates#

Everything in this module has built toward this lesson. You have an opportunity, a platform choice, a workflow map, a function comparison, and named owners. This lesson turns all of it into two things you could actually hand to a sponsor: a one-page pilot charter that’s genuinely bounded, and a scorecard with real thresholds that will tell you, on a specific date, whether to continue, change, or stop.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Define a bounded pilot’s scope, authority, duration, and — critically — its exit criteria
  • Distinguish a bounded pilot from a quiet, unbounded rollout wearing the word “pilot”
  • Select 3–5 KPIs spanning business outcome, task quality, safety, adoption, and cost
  • Connect offline evaluation evidence (like Evaluating Agents’s test-set pass rate) to online pilot evidence, and set the thresholds that trigger a decision

What Makes a Pilot Actually Bounded#

“We’re piloting it” is sometimes true and sometimes a euphemism for “we’re rolling it out without calling it that.” The difference is whether these are all explicitly defined before the pilot starts:

  • User group — a specific, named, small set of users — not “everyone, to start.”
  • Workflow scope — the narrow slice from your Lesson 5 map, not the whole process.
  • Authority limits — the specific authority levels from Lesson 5’s integration map; a pilot doesn’t quietly grant more authority than the design called for.
  • Representative cases — the pilot should see a real mix of the cases the workflow actually produces, not just the easy ones.
  • Exclusions — what’s explicitly out of scope, including any case types staying fully manual (like Lesson 5’s exception queue).
  • Duration — a specific end date, not “until we feel done.”
  • Baseline — the “before” numbers you’re comparing against, measured before the pilot starts.
  • Monitoring — who watches what, how often, during the pilot itself — not just at the end.
  • Human override — how a person stops or corrects the agent mid-instance, in real time.
  • Rollback — the specific, tested mechanism to turn the pilot off entirely and return to the prior process.
  • Incident route — who gets notified, and how fast, if something goes wrong.
  • Exit criteria — the specific, pre-agreed conditions under which the pilot ends — for any of three reasons: success (evidence supports continuing), failure (evidence supports stopping), or the fixed duration simply running out.

A design missing duration, rollback, or exit criteria isn’t a bounded pilot — it’s a deployment that hasn’t been asked hard questions yet. Write all twelve down before anyone starts using the agent.

Choosing KPIs That Span the Real Risk#

Evaluating Agents gave you the technical mechanism — a test set, an LLM-as-judge, a pass rate you track over time. That mechanism answers one question well: is the agent’s output good, on cases like the ones in the test set? A pilot decision needs more than that single number. Select 3–5 KPIs spanning five categories:

  • Business outcome — the metric from your Agent Opportunity Canvas (Lesson 2) — time-to-route, cost per case, whatever you originally set out to move.
  • Task quality — an evaluation-harness pass rate, or a domain-reviewer sampling score, on real pilot cases (not just your original offline test set).
  • Safety — a rate that would matter if it went wrong: an incorrect action taken, a policy violation, an escalation that should have happened but didn’t.
  • Adoption / human experience — are the frontline users (Lesson 10) actually using it, overriding it constructively, or quietly working around it?
  • Latency and cost — is it fast enough and affordable enough at pilot scale to make sense at a larger scale, if it works?

Pick one or two per category, not one per bullet point — five to seven KPIs tracked shallowly is worse than three to five tracked well. The point of spanning categories is that a pilot which improves the business-outcome number while quietly degrading safety or adoption isn’t actually working, even though the headline metric looks good.

Connecting Offline Tests to Online Pilot Evidence#

Your evaluation harness’s pass rate, measured before the pilot starts, is offline evidence — it tells you how the agent performs against a fixed test set you built. The pilot itself generates online evidence — how it performs against real, live cases, including ones your test set never anticipated. Both matter, and they answer different questions:

  • A high offline pass rate + a low online safety metric tells you your test set is missing the failure modes real usage is surfacing — go back and add those real failures to the test set (exactly as Evaluating Agents recommends), don’t just trust the offline number.
  • A low offline pass rate is a signal to fix the agent before running a pilot at all — don’t let a live pilot substitute for the cheaper, faster offline evaluation loop.
  • A good offline score sustained by good online metrics across a real pilot’s duration is the strongest evidence you can bring to the decision gate — it’s held up against both a fixed test and the real, messy world.

The Decision Gate#

Set your thresholds before the pilot starts, not after you’ve seen the results — thresholds chosen in hindsight tend to justify whatever answer people already wanted.

A useful decision gate has three real outcomes, not just “worked” or “didn’t”:

  • Continue — thresholds met or exceeded; move to a larger pilot or limited production, likely with expanded authority only where evidence supports it.
  • Change — mixed results; a specific, named change (narrower scope, more oversight, a different platform, more training) addresses a specific gap, then re-test.
  • Stop — thresholds not met, and no specific change is likely to fix it within a reasonable timeframe; retire the pilot, preserve what was learned, and say so plainly rather than letting it quietly fade.

Worked Example: Cascade Analytics’ Charter and Scorecard#

Continuing the Cascade Analytics example.

Pilot charter (excerpt): User group — the six triage staff. Workflow scope — category and priority recommendation only, per Lesson 5’s map. Authority — recommend, confirmed by a person. Exclusions — the 8% exception-queue tickets stay fully manual. Duration — 6 weeks. Baseline — 40-minute average time-to-route, measured over the prior 2 weeks. Rollback — a single config flag turns recommendations off; staff revert to manual triage with zero data migration needed. Exit criteria — continue if time-to-route drops below 15 minutes with no safety-KPI regression; stop if the override rate exceeds 40% (meaning staff don’t trust the recommendations) or if any billing-data handling incident occurs.

Every KPI clears its continue threshold — a genuinely defensible “continue” recommendation, not a hopeful one, because the thresholds were set before anyone knew the answer.

Practice Exercises#

Exercise 1: Write Your Pilot Charter and Scorecard#

  • Complete the one-page pilot charter for your own opportunity.
  • Select 3–5 KPIs spanning the five categories, and set a continue and a stop threshold for each — before you have any real data to be tempted by.

Common Failure Modes#

  • No duration or exit criteria. A pilot that never explicitly ends just becomes production by default, without ever passing an evaluation.
  • Thresholds set after seeing early results. This isn’t evidence-based decision-making anymore — it’s confirmation bias with a spreadsheet.
  • All KPIs from one category. Five business-outcome metrics and nothing on safety or adoption misses exactly the failure mode that matters most — looking successful while quietly causing harm or losing user trust.
  • Treating “change” as a hidden third option nobody actually plans for. If the evidence is mixed, “change and re-test” needs the same rigor as continue/stop — a vague “we’ll tweak it” is not a plan.
  • No rollback actually tested before the pilot starts. A rollback mechanism that’s never been exercised is a hope, not a control — test it before you need it.

What Goes Into Your Pilot Dossier Today?#

Today’s artifacts: one-page pilot charter, and pilot scorecard with decision gate. These become the bounded-pilot-charter and KPIs-thresholds-decision-rules components of your Pilot Dossier — arguably its most concrete pieces, since they’re what turn everything from Lessons 1–10 into something you can actually run and be judged by.

Summary#

A bounded pilot is defined by twelve explicit elements — user group, scope, authority, representative cases, exclusions, duration, baseline, monitoring, override, rollback, incident route, and exit criteria — written down before it starts, not discovered along the way. KPIs should span business outcome, task quality, safety, adoption, and cost, connecting the offline evaluation-harness evidence from earlier in this course to real online pilot evidence. Thresholds set in advance, for continue, change, and stop, are what turn a pilot’s results into a decision instead of a debate.

Key Concepts#

  • Twelve-element pilot charter — the specific fields that separate a bounded pilot from a quiet rollout.
  • Five KPI categories — business outcome, task quality, safety, adoption, cost.
  • Offline vs. online evidence — a fixed test set vs. real pilot usage; both matter, and gaps between them are informative.
  • Pre-set thresholds — continue, change, or stop, decided before the data exists to bias the decision.

Why This Matters#

This is where the module stops being planning and starts being something you run. A pilot with a real charter and a real scorecard is falsifiable — it can actually fail the test, which is what makes a “continue” recommendation mean something. Everything from the opportunity canvas through the role map exists to make this charter and this scorecard defensible, specific, and honest.
