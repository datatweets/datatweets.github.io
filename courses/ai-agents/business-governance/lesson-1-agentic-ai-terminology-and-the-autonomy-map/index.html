Welcome to Agentic AI Terminology and the Autonomy Map#

Every vendor at every AI conference this year calls their product an “agent.” Half of them mean a chatbot with a system prompt. A quarter mean a scripted workflow with an LLM step bolted on. The rest mean something that genuinely decides what to do next and acts on it. If you can’t tell these apart, you can’t evaluate a vendor claim, size a real risk, or explain to your own leadership what you’re actually proposing to adopt.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish generative AI, a chatbot, an automated workflow, an AI assistant, and an agent by what each one is allowed to decide
  • Explain tools, state, goals, and feedback in plain, non-technical language
  • Place any AI system — a vendor’s or your own — on a concrete autonomy scale, without relying on marketing language
  • Produce an Autonomy Map that states, precisely, how much independence a specific system has today

This lesson is the vocabulary layer under everything else in this course. Every later lesson — the platform decision, the integration map, the threat register, the governance plan — assumes you can already tell a genuine agent from a well-marketed script. Get this wrong and every later document inherits the confusion.

Five Terms, One Confusion#

These five terms describe five genuinely different things, and the difference is not a technicality — it changes who’s accountable when something goes wrong.

TermWhat it actually isWhat it decides
Generative AIA model that produces new text, images, code, or other content from a prompt.Nothing beyond its own output — it has no memory of past calls and takes no action in the world.
ChatbotA conversational interface, often backed by generative AI, that responds to messages in a back-and-forth.What to say next in the conversation — still no action outside the chat window.
Automated workflowA fixed, predetermined sequence of steps — “if X then step 2, else step 3” — that may call an AI model for one step.Nothing at runtime; every branch was decided by whoever built the workflow, in advance.
AI assistantA system that uses AI to help a person complete a task — drafts, suggests, retrieves — but a person reviews and acts.What to suggest; the person still decides what actually happens.
AgentA system that pursues a goal by choosing its own steps — which tool to call, in what order, whether to retry — based on what it observes, within limits someone set.Its own next action, inside boundaries a person defined in advance.

Notice the pattern: moving down the list, the system makes more of the moment-to-moment decisions, and the person who built it moves from deciding each step to deciding the boundaries within which the system decides. That shift — from “I decided the steps” to “I decided the boundaries” — is the entire concept of agentic AI, and it’s also exactly where the risk moves too.

A demo that impresses you might be any of the five

A slick demo where an AI “handles customer support end to end” could be a genuine agent choosing which tool to call for each ticket — or it could be five fixed workflow branches with a chatbot glued to the front. Both can look identical in a five-minute demo. The way to tell them apart isn’t watching the demo again; it’s asking what happens with an input the demo didn’t show you.

Tools, State, Goals, and Feedback — In Plain Language#

An agent (as opposed to the other four) is built from four ingredients. You’ll see these formalized later in this course’s more technical material; here’s what each one means in terms a non-technical stakeholder can actually use:

  • Tools. Things the agent can do — search a database, send an email, call an API, write a file. A system with no tools can only talk; it can’t act. The number and power of an agent’s tools is the single biggest lever on how much damage a mistake can cause.
  • State. What the agent remembers between steps — the conversation so far, facts it looked up, a task list it’s working through. No state means it can’t handle anything that takes more than one step; the wrong kind of state (remembering things it shouldn’t) is a privacy and security question, not just an engineering one.
  • Goals. What the agent is trying to accomplish — not the literal next word to output, but the outcome it’s pursuing across multiple steps. A goal that’s vague (“help the customer”) gives the agent more room to interpret than a goal that’s precise (“resolve this billing dispute using only the refund tool, escalate anything over $200”).
  • Feedback. What the agent notices about the result of its own actions, and what it does differently because of it — retrying a failed call, asking for clarification, stopping when it’s uncertain. A system with no feedback loop will confidently repeat a mistake; this is also where “knowing when to stop” — covered in the more technical What Is an Agent? lesson — becomes a real safety property, not just a nice-to-have.

You do not need to be able to build any of these to use them in a business conversation. You need to be able to ask, about any system someone proposes: what tools does it have, what does it remember, what’s it actually trying to achieve, and how does it know when it’s done or wrong? A vendor who can’t answer all four hasn’t built an agent — or hasn’t thought hard enough about the one they built.

Bounded Autonomy, Without the Hype#

“Autonomous” gets used to mean everything from “runs without a person clicking a button each time” to “makes million-dollar decisions with no oversight at all.” Neither extreme is what a responsible pilot looks like. The useful version of the word is bounded autonomy: the agent decides its own steps, but only inside limits a person set in advance — which tools it may call, what it may never do, how much it may spend, and when it must stop and ask.

Bounded autonomy is not a weaker version of “real” autonomy you’re settling for temporarily. It’s the actual design goal. An agent with no bounds isn’t more capable — it’s just unaccountable, and unaccountable systems are the ones that produce the incidents in Day 8’s incident-response work. Every credible production agent, including the most capable ones in use today, operates inside boundaries someone chose on purpose.

The Autonomy Map#

Use this to describe exactly how much independence a specific system — a vendor’s, or one you’re evaluating — actually has. Fill in one row per system.

FieldWhat to write
SystemThe specific product or proposed agent — not “the AI,” name it.
CategoryGenerative AI, chatbot, automated workflow, AI assistant, or agent — from the table above.
Tools it can callList every action it can actually take, not what it can talk about.
What it remembersNothing, one conversation, or persistent memory across sessions — and for how long.
What it’s trying to achieveThe stated goal, in one sentence — vague goals are a flag, not a feature.
What happens on failureDoes it retry, ask a person, or fail silently?
Who can override it, and howThe specific person or role, and the specific mechanism — not “a human is in the loop” with no further detail.
What it is never allowed to doThe explicit boundary — the thing that, if it happened, would be an incident.

That last row is the one vendors’ marketing pages never fill in, and the one that actually tells you whether you’re looking at bounded autonomy or a demo with no edges.

Worked Example: Cascade Analytics Evaluates a Vendor Claim#

The following is a hypothetical, composite example for teaching purposes — not a real company or a documented case study.

A vendor pitches Cascade Analytics an “AI agent” for expense-report approval. The demo shows it reading a receipt, checking it against policy, and approving it — end to end, no clicks. Before signing anything, Cascade’s evaluator runs it through the Autonomy Map:

  • System: VendorCo Expense Agent v2.
  • Category: On questioning, it turns out to be an automated workflow with one generative-AI step (reading the receipt image) — every branch after that (“if under $50 and matches a known vendor, approve”) was fixed by VendorCo’s engineers, not decided by the system at runtime.
  • Tools it can call: Read receipt image, check three fixed rules, write an approval or rejection to the expense system.
  • What it remembers: Nothing between expense reports.
  • What it’s trying to achieve: Not applicable in any meaningful sense — there’s no goal being pursued, only fixed rules being applied.
  • What happens on failure: An unreadable receipt or an edge case outside the three rules routes to a person; no retry logic.
  • Who can override it, and how: Any finance manager, by rejecting the entry in the expense system before payment.
  • What it is never allowed to do: Approve anything over the fixed dollar thresholds — this isn’t a bounded-autonomy decision, it’s just outside what the fixed rules cover.

The finding: this is a well-built automated workflow, genuinely useful, and considerably lower-risk than an agent would be — but it is not an agent, and Cascade now knows to evaluate it, staff it, and govern it as what it actually is, not as the marketing term on the vendor’s slide.

Exercise: Map an AI System You’ve Actually Encountered#

Pick one AI system — a vendor pitch, a tool your organization already uses, or a well-known consumer product. Fill in the Autonomy Map completely, including the “never allowed to do” row. If you genuinely don’t know the answer to a row, write “unknown — ask the vendor” rather than guessing; that gap is itself useful information about how well the system has actually been specified.

Common Failure Modes#

  • Accepting “agent” as a category, not a claim. Every “agent” claim should be checked against the five-way table above — many resolve to automated workflows or assistants once you ask what actually decides the next step.
  • Confusing more tools with more autonomy. A system with ten tools and a fixed script for using them is still an automated workflow; a system with one tool but genuine discretion over when to use it is closer to an agent. Count decisions, not tool integrations.
  • Treating “human in the loop” as a complete answer. Which human, reviewing what, with the power to do what about it, are three separate questions a real Autonomy Map answers explicitly.
  • Leaving the “never allowed to do” row blank. A system with no stated boundary hasn’t been designed for bounded autonomy — it’s just been left open, whether or not anyone intended that.

What Goes Into Your Pilot Dossier Today?#

Today’s artifact: Agentic AI terminology and autonomy map. This becomes the strategic-framing foundation of your Pilot Dossier — every later lesson refers back to the vocabulary and the autonomy boundary you establish here. Tomorrow you’ll use this same precision to name a specific, bounded opportunity in your own organization.

Summary#

Generative AI, chatbots, automated workflows, AI assistants, and agents are five different things, distinguished by what decides the next step — the system, or the person who built it, in advance. An agent’s four ingredients — tools, state, goals, and feedback — translate directly into a business question: what can it do, what does it remember, what’s it pursuing, and how does it know when it’s wrong? Bounded autonomy — real independence inside limits a person set on purpose — is the actual design goal, not a compromise, and the Autonomy Map is how you state those limits precisely enough to evaluate, govern, and hold anyone accountable for.

Key Concepts#

  • Generative AI vs. chatbot vs. automated workflow vs. AI assistant vs. agent — distinguished by what decides the next step.
  • Tools, state, goals, feedback — the four ingredients that make a system an agent rather than a script.
  • Bounded autonomy — genuine independence inside limits someone set on purpose, not weaker autonomy or an unaccountable free-for-all.
  • Autonomy Map — states a system’s tools, memory, goal, failure behavior, override mechanism, and explicit boundary, precisely enough to evaluate.

Why This Matters#

Every claim you’ll evaluate for the rest of this course — a vendor’s, a colleague’s, your own — arrives wearing the word “agent” whether or not it’s earned it. The five-way distinction and the Autonomy Map are how you cut through that immediately, in the first five minutes of any pitch, rather than discovering the real category six months into a deployment.
