Welcome to AI Agents Across Business Functions#

Everything so far has focused on one workflow. This lesson widens the view: comparing opportunities across business functions, so you can see where agentic AI is easiest to try safely, where it’s genuinely valuable but needs more care, and where a first pilot has no business going at all. The scenarios below are original and deliberately hypothetical — not case studies, not promises, not a ranking of what you should build first.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Compare candidate use cases across eight business functions
  • Explain why acceptable autonomy differs sharply by function, even for similar-looking tasks
  • Recognize which categories of decision don’t belong in a casual first pilot
  • Score a set of opportunities into a business-function opportunity portfolio with value, feasibility, and risk columns

Eight Functions, One Pattern Each#

Every scenario below is a hypothetical, composite illustration — not a real organization, and not a documented result. Each stays intentionally bounded: augmentation or narrow bounded automation, never full autonomy over a consequential decision.

  • Customer service — drafting support replies grounded in documentation, with citations, for a human agent to review before sending. Risk shape: wrong information reaching a customer; mitigated by grounding discipline (Module 4) and a human-in-the-loop draft stage.
  • Operations — drafting exception-handling responses for a logistics queue, with a person approving anything that changes an order or a shipment. Risk shape: an incorrect action with real-world, physical, or financial consequence; mitigated by keeping “execute” authority off the table until the recommend/draft stages have real evidence.
  • Product — clustering and summarizing user feedback into candidate themes for a human product team to prioritize. Risk shape: low — a miscategorized theme wastes a little attention, not a decision with lasting consequence.
  • Marketing — generating on-brand content variants for a human editor to select and approve before anything publishes. Risk shape: brand and reputational, not physical or financial; mitigated by keeping publish authority entirely human.
  • Software / data — drafting a first-pass root-cause note from logs and recent deploys for an engineer to confirm or reject. Risk shape: low-to-moderate — a wrong hypothesis wastes investigation time but doesn’t itself change production systems.
  • Finance / procurement — flagging invoice or expense anomalies for a reviewer; never auto-approving a payment. Risk shape: high — financial loss or fraud exposure; this is exactly the kind of decision that stays at “recommend,” not “execute,” for a long time, if ever.
  • Knowledge work — retrieving and summarizing internal policy documents for a first-pass answer a specialist verifies before it’s used. Risk shape: moderate — an ungrounded answer treated as authoritative; mitigated by the grounding-and-citations discipline from Module 4.
  • People operations — drafting onboarding-document answers for HR to confirm. Risk shape: high if scope creeps — employment, compensation, and disciplinary decisions must stay explicitly out of a first pilot’s scope, full stop.

What never belongs in a casual first pilot

Employment decisions (hiring, firing, performance ratings), health decisions, credit or lending decisions, insurance underwriting, and legal determinations share a pattern: they’re high-stakes for the individual affected, often legally regulated, and wrong far less forgivingly than a mis-routed support ticket. If a business-function opportunity touches any of these, it needs specialist legal, compliance, and domain review before a pilot conversation even starts — not as a formality, but because the consequences of getting it wrong land on a real person, not just on your project timeline. None of the eight scenarios above cross that line; keep it that way when you write your own.

Why Acceptable Autonomy Differs by Function#

Two tasks that look structurally similar — “read some text, produce a categorization” — can warrant very different autonomy levels depending on function. A miscategorized product-feedback theme costs someone a few minutes of misdirected attention. A miscategorized expense report that gets auto-approved costs real money and potentially enables fraud. The task shape is similar; the consequence of being wrong is not.

Three questions consistently separate “this function tolerates more autonomy” from “this function needs more oversight”:

  • Is the error cheap and easy to notice, or expensive and easy to miss? A wrong content variant a human never selects costs nothing; a wrong invoice approval that goes unnoticed costs money.
  • Is the affected party inside your organization (an employee reviewing output) or outside it (a customer, a vendor, a regulator)? Errors reaching outside your organization are harder to quietly correct and carry more reputational weight.
  • Is the decision reversible? A published social post is hard to fully unpublish; a draft an editor rejects before publishing is trivially reversible.

Apply these three questions to any function-specific opportunity, not just the eight above — they generalize better than a fixed “this function is always safe / this function is always risky” rule would.

The Business-Function Opportunity Portfolio#

Score candidate opportunities — your own, across functions you actually have visibility into — on three dimensions, 1 (low) to 5 (high):

  • Value — how much would this actually move a real metric, if it worked?
  • Feasibility — given today’s platform, data, and integration realities (Lessons 3 and 5), how achievable is this now?
  • Risk — how costly and how reversible is a wrong output, using the three questions above?
FunctionOpportunity (one line)Value (1-5)Feasibility (1-5)Risk (1-5, higher = riskier)

High value, high feasibility, low risk is the sweet spot for a first pilot — not necessarily the highest-value idea on the list. An opportunity with value 5 and risk 5 might be the right long-term target, but it’s rarely the right first pilot; Module 6 will have you choose deliberately, with this portfolio as the input, not just default to the top-value row.

Worked Example: A Four-Row Portfolio#

Continuing the hypothetical composite style from earlier lessons — not a real organization’s actual results.

FunctionOpportunityValueFeasibilityRisk
Customer serviceDraft grounded replies to common billing questions442
FinanceFlag anomalous expense reports for review435
ProductCluster user feedback into themes weekly351
MarketingGenerate email subject line variants for A/B testing251

Reading this portfolio: customer service scores well on all three and is a strong first-pilot candidate. Finance scores high on value but also on risk — worth pursuing eventually, with real governance (Module 7) in place first, not as a first pilot. Product feedback clustering is low-risk and highly feasible but modest value — a reasonable “quick win” that builds organizational trust in agentic AI without much downside, even if it’s not the headline initiative. Marketing subject lines are similar: cheap to try, low stakes, unlikely to be the transformation story, useful as an early, low-friction success.

Exercise: Build Your Own Portfolio#

List 3–5 real or plausible opportunities across at least three different business functions you have visibility into. Score each on value, feasibility, and risk. Identify which row is your strongest first-pilot candidate — and write one sentence on why it beats the highest-value row, if it isn’t the same row.

Common Failure Modes#

  • Picking the highest-value row as the first pilot by default. Value alone ignores feasibility and risk — the best first pilot is usually the best combination of the three, not the single biggest number.
  • Scoring risk from the task’s description instead of its consequence. “Draft a reply” sounds low-risk regardless of function; the consequence of a wrong draft reaching a customer versus reaching an internal reviewer is what actually differs.
  • Letting one function’s success justify skipping evaluation elsewhere. A working customer-service pilot doesn’t mean a finance opportunity is automatically safe at the same authority level — each function’s risk profile gets assessed on its own.
  • Including a high-stakes function without flagging it. If a real opportunity does touch employment, health, credit, insurance, or legal decisions, name that explicitly and route it to the right specialists — don’t quietly score it like any other row.

What Goes Into Your Pilot Dossier Today?#

Today’s artifact: business-function opportunity portfolio. This becomes the functional-opportunity-portfolio component of your Pilot Dossier. Even if your actual pilot only ever touches one function, this portfolio shows a stakeholder you evaluated the field before committing — and it’s exactly what tomorrow’s organizational-change lesson and Module 6’s pilot design will draw the strongest candidate from.

Summary#

Agentic AI opportunities exist across every business function, but risk and acceptable autonomy vary sharply between them — not because the tasks look different, but because the consequences of a wrong output do. Three questions (cheap-and-noticeable vs. expensive-and-missed error, internal vs. external impact, reversible vs. not) separate functions that tolerate more autonomy from those that need tighter oversight. A small set of high-stakes categories — employment, health, credit, insurance, legal — don’t belong in a casual first pilot at all. Scoring candidate opportunities on value, feasibility, and risk turns “which function should we start with” into a comparison instead of a hunch.

Key Concepts#

  • Risk shape varies by function — same task structure, different consequence of being wrong.
  • Three risk questions — cost/noticeability of error, internal vs. external impact, reversibility.
  • Excluded categories — employment, health, credit, insurance, and legal decisions need specialist review before a pilot conversation, not a portfolio row.
  • Value/feasibility/risk portfolio — the best first pilot combines all three, not just the highest value.

Why This Matters#

An organization’s first agentic AI pilot sets the tone for every one after it — a rushed pilot in a high-risk function that goes wrong can set the whole initiative back years; a well-chosen pilot in a lower-risk function that clearly works builds the case, the skills, and the trust to tackle harder ones later. Comparing across functions, honestly, before committing, is what makes that first choice a deliberate one instead of whichever idea happened to reach you first.
