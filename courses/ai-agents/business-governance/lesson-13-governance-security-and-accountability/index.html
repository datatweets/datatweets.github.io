Welcome to Governance, Security, and Accountability#

Every control you’ve designed so far — authority limits, rollback, human review, KPIs — protects the pilot while you’re paying close attention to it. Governance is what keeps those controls working after the excitement of launch fades, the original team moves on, and the agent quietly becomes “just part of how we do this now.” This lesson connects your pilot’s specific controls to durable accountability: who decides, what evidence proves the controls are working, and what triggers a re-review.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Classify a use case by consequence and autonomy, and explain why that classification drives everything else
  • Identify affected stakeholders and name which internal or external requirements plausibly apply
  • Assign real decision rights and build a control/evidence matrix
  • Set review triggers for when the model, data, tools, or workflow change
  • Explain the difference between a voluntary framework, a management-system standard, and binding law — and why none of them substitutes for qualified legal advice

Classify by Consequence and Autonomy#

Two questions set the governance bar for any use case:

  • Consequence — if this goes wrong, how bad is it, for whom? A miscategorized product-feedback theme and a wrongly-approved expense report are not the same consequence tier, even if both are “the agent made a mistake.”
  • Autonomy — how much can the agent do without a person in the loop, right now, at this stage of the pilot? Recall Lesson 5’s five levels — read, recommend, draft, approve, execute.
Low autonomy (read/recommend)High autonomy (approve/execute)
Low consequenceLight-touch governance — document it, review occasionallyModerate — the autonomy itself warrants attention even if each instance is low-stakes
High consequenceModerate — a human is still deciding, but the agent’s recommendation carries real weightHighest tier — the most rigorous controls, evidence, and review cadence apply here

A use case’s tier isn’t fixed forever — it changes if you expand authority, change the population it affects, or extend it to a new function. Re-classify whenever a Lesson 11-style change happens, not just once at pilot launch.

Stakeholders and Applicable Requirements#

List everyone affected — not just direct users, but anyone whose data, work, or outcomes the use case touches (Lesson 10’s frontline users, Lesson 2’s affected-users field, and anyone external like customers or vendors).

Then name which internal and external requirements plausibly apply — as a prompt to investigate, not a legal determination you’re making yourself:

  • Internal policy — data handling standards, existing security requirements, procurement rules for new vendors.
  • Data protection law — regulations like the EU’s GDPR or various US state privacy laws may apply depending on whose data you process and where you and they are located.
  • Sector-specific law — healthcare (e.g., HIPAA in the US), financial services, and other regulated sectors carry additional rules that apply based on your industry and the specific data involved.
  • Employment and consumer law — decisions that affect employees or consumers as such may trigger specific protections, particularly for anything resembling automated decision-making about a person.
  • Emerging AI-specific regulation — frameworks like the EU AI Act introduce risk-based obligations that vary by what the system does and who it affects.

This is a prompt to ask an expert, not an answer

Whether any specific law applies to your specific use case depends on your jurisdiction, your organization’s role, the data involved, and the exact use case — questions this lesson cannot answer for you, and a general course shouldn’t try to. Treat this list as your cue to involve qualified legal, privacy, security, HR, procurement, or sector experts before a use case goes live, not as a checklist you can complete yourself and call it compliant.

Decision Rights and the Control/Evidence Matrix#

Decision rights name who can actually authorize each kind of change — not who’s informed, who decides:

  • Who can expand the agent’s authority level at an integration point?
  • Who can approve extending the pilot to a new user group or function?
  • Who can pause or roll back the pilot, and does that person need anyone else’s sign-off to act immediately?
  • Who owns the risk tier classification, and re-classifies when something changes?

The control/evidence matrix connects each significant risk to a specific control and the evidence that proves the control is actually working — not just that it exists on paper:

RiskControlEvidence it’s working
(from your Module 4 threat register)(specific mitigation)(a log, a metric, an audit — something checkable)

Review Triggers#

Governance isn’t a one-time sign-off — set explicit triggers for when a use case needs re-review, not just a calendar cadence:

  • Model change — the underlying model is upgraded or swapped; behavior can shift in ways your original evaluation didn’t test.
  • Data change — a new data source is added, or the population the agent’s data covers changes.
  • Tool change — a new tool is added, or an existing tool’s own behavior or permissions change.
  • Workflow change — the agent is extended to a new user group, a new function, or a higher authority level than originally approved.
  • Calendar cadence — even with no trigger event, a fixed review interval (e.g., quarterly) catches drift nothing else would flag.
Prevent Least privilege, allowlists, approval gates
Detect Monitoring, logs, anomaly signals
Respond Contain, correct, communicate
Recover Rollback, evidence, feed lessons back to Prevent

The prevent/detect/respond/recover loop above is the operational complement to this lesson’s governance structure: prevention is what Module 4’s controls and this lesson’s decision rights establish before anything goes wrong; detect/respond/recover is what your monitoring, incident route (Lesson 11’s charter), and this lesson’s review triggers handle once something does — and every recovery should feed back into strengthening prevention, closing the loop rather than just resetting to where you started.

Voluntary Framework, Management Standard, or Binding Law?#

These are three different kinds of thing, and conflating them leads to real mistakes:

  • NIST AI RMF (a voluntary framework) organizes risk management into four functions — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage — and its Generative AI Profile extends that specifically to generative-AI risks. It’s a way of organizing your thinking, not a certification you obtain.
  • OECD AI principles are high-level, voluntary principles — including transparency, robustness/security/safety, and accountability — that many national AI policies reference, but they don’t themselves create binding obligations on your organization.
  • ISO/IEC 42001 is a management-system standard — a structured way an organization can run and continually improve its AI governance, similar in spirit to how ISO 27001 works for information security. An organization can pursue formal certification against it; simply reading about it doesn’t confer one, and DATATWEETS’ own course-completion certificate (see this campaign’s certificate of completion) is unrelated to ISO/IEC 42001 certification.
  • Actual law — GDPR, sector regulation, employment law, the EU AI Act, and similar — is binding, varies by jurisdiction and use case, and carries real consequences for non-compliance. This is the one category where “we followed a voluntary framework” is not itself a legal defense.

Use the frameworks and the standard as conceptual anchors for how you organize governance work — they’re genuinely useful for structuring the control/evidence matrix and review cadence above. Treat law as a separate, mandatory track that needs its own qualified review, informed by but not satisfied by anything in this lesson.

Worked Example: Cascade Analytics’ Governance Plan#

Continuing the Cascade Analytics example.

Classification: Moderate consequence (routing errors are recoverable but real; billing-adjacent customer data is involved), low-to-moderate autonomy (recommend only, human confirms). Tier: moderate — real controls and evidence required, but not the highest-tier scrutiny a payment-approval agent would need.

Stakeholders: Triage staff, customers whose tickets are routed, the CRM data these tickets touch. Requirements to investigate: internal data-handling policy (confirmed applicable — customer account data is in scope); data protection law relevant to wherever their customers are located (flagged for the IT security lead, who part-owns this role per Lesson 10, to confirm with legal).

Decision rights: Product owner (customer success director) approves any authority-level change; process owner (triage lead) can pause the pilot immediately, no sign-off required; executive sponsor hears the Module 6 continue/change/stop recommendation.

Control/evidence matrix (excerpt):

RiskControlEvidence
Agent recommends wrong category, undetectedWeekly domain-reviewer spot-check (Lesson 10)Spot-check log, reviewed monthly by the risk lead
Customer PII exposed in logsLogs store ticket ID and category only, not full ticket textLog schema review, confirmed by security lead at launch

Review triggers: Model upgrade; CRM data schema change; any extension beyond the six-person pilot group; quarterly calendar review regardless.

Exercise: Build Your Own Governance Plan#

For your pilot, classify it by consequence and autonomy, list stakeholders and requirements to investigate (not answer yourself), name decision rights for at least three kinds of change, build a control/evidence matrix with at least two rows drawn from your own threat thinking, and list your review triggers.

Common Failure Modes#

  • Treating a voluntary framework as compliance. Following NIST AI RMF’s structure is good practice; it is not the same as satisfying GDPR, sector regulation, or any binding law that applies to your use case.
  • A control with no evidence. “We have an approval step” isn’t evidence it’s working — a log showing approvals actually happened, reviewed by someone, is.
  • No re-classification trigger. A use case that started low-risk and grew in scope without anyone re-checking its risk tier is a use case that’s outgrown its original governance.
  • Decision rights that exist on paper but aren’t exercised. If the person who “can pause the pilot” has never actually tested doing so, that’s not a real control yet.
  • Skipping qualified review because the team feels confident. Internal confidence is not a substitute for legal, privacy, or sector expertise on a use case that plausibly triggers real regulatory requirements.

What Goes Into Your Pilot Dossier Today?#

Today’s artifact: governance plan — risk tier, accountability, control/evidence matrix, testing plan, documentation set, review cadence, and escalation. This becomes the governance-accountability-and-evidence-plan component of your Pilot Dossier, and it’s what Day 14’s ethics and organizational-impact review will draw on most directly.

Summary#

Governance classifies a use case by consequence and autonomy, names the stakeholders and the requirements worth investigating with real experts, assigns decision rights to specific people, and builds a control/evidence matrix that proves controls are working rather than just existing on paper. Review triggers — model, data, tool, or workflow change, plus a calendar cadence — keep the classification current as the pilot evolves. NIST AI RMF, OECD AI principles, and ISO/IEC 42001 are useful conceptual anchors for organizing this work; none of them, nor this lesson, substitutes for qualified legal review of the law that actually applies to your use case.

Key Concepts#

  • Consequence × autonomy — the two-axis classification that sets the governance bar.
  • Decision rights — who can actually authorize a change, not just who’s informed.
  • Control/evidence matrix — every risk paired with a control and the evidence it’s working.
  • Review triggers — model, data, tool, workflow changes, plus a fixed calendar cadence.
  • Framework vs. standard vs. law — voluntary guidance, a certifiable management system, and binding legal obligation are three different things.

Why This Matters#

A pilot with excellent technical controls and no governance structure works right up until the person who built it leaves, the model gets upgraded, or the use case quietly expands — at which point nobody notices the original assumptions no longer hold. Governance is what makes today’s careful design still hold a year from now, under a different team, at a different scale.
