Welcome to Workflows, Digital Ecosystems, and Integrations#

An agent that looks perfect in a demo can fail entirely once it meets your real systems — the ticketing tool with no public API, the approval step that lives in someone’s inbox, the exception case nobody documented because everyone just knows to escalate it. This lesson teaches you to map a workflow completely enough that none of that is a surprise, then to design exactly where and how an agent connects to it.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Model a current-state workflow’s trigger, inputs, decisions, systems, data, actions, people, exceptions, and outputs
  • Identify every system and data source an agent implementation would actually touch
  • Assign one of five authority levels — read, recommend, draft, approve, execute — at each integration point
  • Produce a current-state workflow and system-context map for your own opportunity

Nine Things Every Workflow Map Needs#

A workflow map that’s missing even one of these categories tends to fail exactly where the gap is. Walk your workflow end to end and name each:

  • Trigger — what starts one instance? A new ticket, a scheduled time, a form submission.
  • Inputs — what information arrives with the trigger, and where does it come from?
  • Decisions — every point a person currently judges something and chooses a path.
  • Systems — every piece of software the workflow touches, by name.
  • Data — what records get read, and what gets written or updated.
  • Actions — what actually happens as a result — a message sent, a record changed, a case closed.
  • People — everyone who does something in this workflow, or is affected by its output.
  • Exceptions — the cases that don’t follow the normal path, and who handles them today.
  • Outputs — what the workflow produces, and who or what consumes it next.

Most workflow descriptions are complete on inputs, decisions, and outputs — because those are what people describe when asked “what do you do?” — and incomplete on systems, data, exceptions, and the full list of affected people, because those are the parts embedded in habit rather than spoken aloud. Interview the person who actually does the work, not just their manager, and ask specifically: “what do you do when it doesn’t go the normal way?”

The Digital Ecosystem an Agent Actually Touches#

Once the workflow is mapped, the ecosystem question is: for each system and data source in that map, what would an agent implementation need?

  • APIs and tools — does the system have a documented API, or would this need screen-scraping or manual export? The Model Context Protocol is worth knowing about here — it’s an emerging standard for connecting an AI application to external tools and data sources in a consistent way, which matters when you’re integrating with more than one or two systems.
  • Retrieval sources — any documents, knowledge bases, or records the agent needs to ground its answers in, not just act on. See Why Agents Need Retrieval if this workflow involves answering from your own content rather than just calling tools.
  • Identity and permissions — does the agent act under its own service identity, or impersonate the triggering user? What can that identity actually access — and critically, is it scoped narrower than a human user’s full access, or does it inherit everything?
  • Logs — where does a record of what the agent did get written, and who can review it?
  • Human reviewers — for each integration point, who is the person in the loop, and what do they actually see before or after the agent acts?

Five Authority Levels#

This is the decision that matters most at each integration point: how much can the agent do there on its own?

LevelWhat it meansExample
ReadThe agent can look, not touch.Retrieves ticket history to inform its own reasoning.
RecommendThe agent suggests; a person decides and acts.Proposes a ticket category; a person clicks to confirm or override.
DraftThe agent produces content a person reviews before it goes anywhere.Writes a reply a support agent edits and sends.
ApproveThe agent’s decision executes unless a person actively stops it within a window.A routing decision applies automatically unless flagged within an hour.
ExecuteThe agent acts directly, no per-instance human step.Auto-closes tickets matching an exact, narrow, pre-approved pattern.

Authority level is chosen per integration point, not once for the whole agent — a single workflow’s agent might read three systems, recommend on one decision, and draft on one output, with nothing at “execute” in a first pilot. Higher authority isn’t a sign of a more sophisticated agent; it’s a claim about how much evidence and trust you have in that specific decision, at that specific point. Lesson 11 (bounded pilots) and Lessons 7–8 (security) both assume you’ve made this call explicitly, point by point, rather than granting broad authority once and hoping it’s fine everywhere.

People Frontline users, reviewers
Systems & data APIs, records, retrieval sources
Agent (bounded) Read, recommend, draft — within scope
Approval gate A person approves before it executes
Logs Every decision, traceable after the fact
Outcome Measured against the baseline

The diagram above is the shape every workflow-plus-agent design should be checkable against: people and systems feed data to a bounded agent, the agent’s proposed action passes through an approval gate appropriate to its authority level, everything gets logged, and the outcome is what you measure — the same evidence chain Lesson 11’s KPIs and Lesson 13’s governance plan will both refer back to.

Worked Example: Cascade Analytics’ Integration Map#

Continuing the Cascade Analytics example from Lessons 2–3.

Cascade Analytics’ support-triage workflow, mapped completely:

  • Trigger: a ticket arrives via the support portal or inbound email.
  • Inputs: ticket text, customer account ID, product tier.
  • Decisions: category (billing/bug/how-to), priority (normal/urgent).
  • Systems: the ticketing platform, the CRM (for account tier), the internal knowledge base.
  • Data read: ticket text, customer account record. Data written: category, priority, routing queue.
  • Actions: ticket routed to the matching queue.
  • People: the six triage staff (task changes), customer success leads (handle escalations), customers (experience the wait).
  • Exceptions: a ticket matching no clear category goes to a general queue a senior triage staffer reviews manually — today, about 8% of volume.
  • Outputs: a routed ticket, visible to the receiving queue’s staff.

Integration and authority, point by point:

Integration pointSystemAuthority
Read ticket + account dataTicketing platform, CRMRead
Propose category + priority(agent’s own reasoning)Recommend
Confirm and routeTicketing platformPerson acts, using the agent’s recommendation
Handle exceptions (unclear category)Ticketing platformUnchanged — stays with the senior staffer
Log every recommendation and outcomeInternal log storeRead/write for audit, not visible to the customer

Notice the exception path is explicitly unchanged — the agent doesn’t attempt the 8% of tickets that don’t fit a clear category; those still go to the person who already handles them well. That’s a deliberate scope boundary, not a gap: extending the agent to exceptions is a decision for a later pilot, with its own evidence, not something that should happen by default because the agent is “already there.”

Practice Exercises#

Exercise 1: Map Your Own Workflow and Ecosystem#

Using the opportunity from Lesson 2:

  • Walk the workflow end to end and fill in all nine categories above.
  • For each system it touches, note whether a documented API exists.
  • Assign an authority level to every integration point — and if any point tempts you toward “execute,” write one sentence justifying why the evidence supports that instead of “recommend” or “draft.”

Common Failure Modes#

  • Mapping the happy path only. A workflow map with no exceptions section is not describing a real workflow — it’s describing the 80–90% of cases nobody has to think about.
  • Assuming an API exists. “We’ll just connect to the system” is not a plan until someone has confirmed a documented, permissioned way to do it — screen-scraping and manual export are real constraints, not implementation details to wave away.
  • One authority level for the whole agent. Treating “the agent’s authority” as a single dial instead of a per-integration-point decision either grants too much where it shouldn’t, or holds back stages that don’t need it.
  • No log at all. Skipping the logging integration point because it doesn’t seem like part of “the workflow” removes the evidence Lesson 13’s governance plan and Lesson 8’s incident response will both need later.
  • Identity that’s broader than the task. Giving the agent’s service identity the same access as a full admin user, because it was the easiest credential to reach for, is a security decision made by convenience rather than by design — Lesson 7 covers exactly this risk.

What Goes Into Your Pilot Dossier Today?#

Today’s artifact: current-state workflow and system-context map. This becomes part of the workflow-and-digital-ecosystem-map component of your Pilot Dossier, alongside tomorrow’s integration and authority design work. Every system, data source, and authority level you named here is what Lesson 7’s threat register and Lesson 13’s governance plan will assess for risk.

Summary#

A workflow map is only useful if it’s complete: trigger, inputs, decisions, systems, data, actions, people, exceptions, and outputs — with exceptions and the full list of affected people the two categories most often left out. From that map, the ecosystem question is what an agent implementation would actually need at each system and data source — APIs, retrieval sources, identity and permissions, logs, and human reviewers. And the authority question — read, recommend, draft, approve, or execute — gets decided per integration point, not once for the whole agent, based on the evidence you actually have.

Key Concepts#

  • Nine-part workflow map — trigger, inputs, decisions, systems, data, actions, people, exceptions, outputs.
  • Digital ecosystem — the APIs, retrieval sources, identity/permissions, logs, and reviewers an agent implementation touches.
  • Five authority levels — read, recommend, draft, approve, execute — assigned per integration point.
  • Exceptions matter most — the cases nobody describes unprompted are usually where a naive implementation breaks.

Why This Matters#

A platform decision and a cost estimate mean little if the agent can’t actually connect to the systems it needs, or if nobody thought through who’s accountable when it acts on partial information. This map is what Lesson 7 (security), Lesson 11 (the pilot charter), and Lesson 13 (governance) all build on — skipping it here means redoing it, under more pressure, once a pilot design turns out to be missing a system nobody mentioned.
