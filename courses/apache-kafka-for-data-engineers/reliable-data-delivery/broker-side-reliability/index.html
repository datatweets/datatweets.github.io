Welcome to Broker-Side Reliability#

Lesson 1 proved delivery guarantees come from a consumer-side ordering decision. This lesson moves to settings that live on the broker itself, outside any single producer or consumer’s control: how many copies of a partition exist at all, and what the cluster is allowed to do when every in-sync copy disappears at once.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain why replication factor, not just acks, determines whether a broker outage is survivable at all
  • Predict the outcome difference between a replication-factor-1 and replication-factor-3 topic during a real outage
  • Define unclean.leader.election.enable’s safety-vs-availability tradeoff precisely
  • Recognize a genuine limit of a combined-mode broker-and-controller cluster like this course’s own

acks and min.insync.replicas: A Two-Line Recap#

Module 5 Lesson 3 already proved these two thoroughly: acks='all' waits for the partition’s high watermark to advance past every in-sync replica, and min.insync.replicas is the floor the broker actively enforces under that wait, triggering a real NotEnoughReplicasException once the ISR drops below it. Nothing about either changes here — they’re the acknowledgment-side controls. This lesson is about the setting neither of them touches: how many replicas can exist to be in sync with in the first place.

Replication Factor Is Reliability’s Foundation, Not a Detail#

acks='all' and min.insync.replicas=2 mean nothing on a topic with only one replica — there’s no second copy to wait for or require. Replication factor sets the ceiling every other reliability setting operates under. Prove it by comparing two topics side by side during the identical outage: one with replication factor 1, one with replication factor 3, both pinned to specific brokers so the outcome is deterministic.

Same broker outage, same moment, two completely different outcomes — and the difference is entirely replication factor. cityflow-rf1-check has exactly one replica, so when its one broker goes down there is nothing left to serve the partition; the send fails outright and stays failed until that specific broker comes back. cityflow-rf3-check has two other replicas that never went down, so a leader was already in place the whole time and the send just succeeds, no different from any single-broker outage Module 5 already covered. Every acks and min.insync.replicas setting this course has used assumed replication factor 3 without saying so out loud; this is what happens when that assumption is removed.

unclean.leader.election.enable: The Setting for When Every In-Sync Replica Is Gone#

Every failover this course has shown so far — Module 5’s broker kills, the RF=3 topic above — elects a new leader from the current ISR, a replica the outgoing leader had already confirmed was caught up. unclean.leader.election.enable governs a narrower, more dangerous case: what happens when the ISR is empty and the only replicas left alive are ones that had already fallen out of sync. Left false (Kafka’s own default, and this cluster’s real configured value, confirmed directly):

With it false, a partition with no in-sync replica available simply goes offline — unavailable, but every acknowledged write stays intact, waiting for an in-sync replica to return. Set it true, and the controller will elect one of the stale, out-of-sync replicas as leader anyway to restore availability — which means every write the old leader had that the stale replica never caught up on is truncated away, silently, the moment the new leader starts serving. It’s a direct trade: false favors correctness over uptime, true favors uptime over correctness. Kafka ships with false as the default for exactly the reason CityFlow would want it here — a taxi trip event that’s simply unavailable for a few minutes is recoverable; one that’s silently gone is not.

A Real Limit of This Course’s Own Cluster#

Demonstrating unclean election live needs a partition with zero in-sync replicas available while at least one out-of-sync replica still exists to elect — which means at least two of this topic’s three replica brokers have to be down at once. On CityFlow’s .kafka-local cluster, every broker also runs the KRaft controller for the same three-node quorum Lesson 1 of Module 5 covered, and that quorum needs a majority — two of three — to make any metadata decision at all, including who leads a partition. Stopping two of three brokers to force an unclean election doesn’t just take down the partition’s replicas; it takes down the controller quorum’s ability to decide anything, unclean election included.

This was tested directly rather than assumed. With kafka1 and kafka3 stopped and only kafka2 left running, the surviving broker’s own log shows exactly what happens instead of a clean unclean election:

kafka2 isn’t electing anything, unclean or otherwise — it’s stuck, repeating “still catching up” forever, because it’s one vote out of three and can never reach the majority a metadata decision requires. This is a genuine property of a combined-mode cluster, where every node is both a data broker and a controller voter: it can safely lose exactly one node at a time and keep functioning, for data and metadata alike. A production deployment typically runs its controller quorum on a small, separate set of nodes (often three or five) from a much larger pool of brokers, which decouples the two — losing two of fifty brokers doesn’t threaten a controller quorum of five. This course’s own three-node lab doesn’t have that separation, and that’s a real, worth-knowing limitation of learning Kafka on a small local cluster rather than a gap in this lesson.

Practice Exercises#

Exercise 1: Restore cityflow-rf1-check to replication factor 3#

kafka-reassign-partitions.sh can add replicas to an existing topic. Look up its --reassignment-json-file usage and reassign cityflow-rf1-check’s single partition onto all three brokers. Confirm with --describe that Isr shows all three once the new replicas finish catching up.

Exercise 2: Find unclean.leader.election.enable’s topic-level override#

This lesson read the setting at the broker level. Create a topic with --config unclean.leader.election.enable=true and confirm with kafka-configs.sh --entity-type topics that the topic-level value overrides the broker default — the same override pattern Module 5 Lesson 2 already used for min.insync.replicas.

Hint

kafka-configs.sh --entity-type topics --entity-name <topic> --describe --all prints a synonyms list showing exactly which level – topic, static broker, or Kafka’s own default – won.

Summary#

Replication factor sets the ceiling every other reliability setting depends on — proven here with an identical outage failing a replication-factor-1 topic outright while a replication-factor-3 topic kept working without interruption. unclean.leader.election.enable governs the narrower, riskier case of what happens when even that redundancy is exhausted: false, the real confirmed default here, refuses to trade correctness for uptime. Attempting to force that scenario live on this course’s own combined-mode cluster didn’t produce a clean unclean election — it revealed a genuine limit of running broker and controller roles on the same three nodes, backed by the cluster’s own real log output rather than assumed.

Key Concepts#

  • Replication factor is the foundation: acks and min.insync.replicas have nothing to enforce on a topic with only one replica.
  • A replication-factor-1 outage has no fallback — the partition is unavailable until that specific broker returns.
  • unclean.leader.election.enable=false (the real default here) favors correctness over uptime when every in-sync replica is gone.
  • A combined-mode cluster’s controller quorum needs a majority — this lab’s 3-node topology can safely lose only one node at a time, for data and metadata alike.

Why This Matters#

Every reliability setting this module covers assumes a cluster that can actually survive a failure in the first place — replication factor is what makes that assumption true or false. Lesson 3 moves to the producer side of reliability next: enable.idempotence, and what it actually prevents when a producer retries against a cluster exactly like the one this lesson just tested the limits of.
