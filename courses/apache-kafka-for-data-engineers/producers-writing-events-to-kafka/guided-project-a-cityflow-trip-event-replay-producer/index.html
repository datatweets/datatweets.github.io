Lesson 5 - Guided Project: A CityFlow Trip-Event Replay Producer

Welcome to This Module’s Guided Project#

Four lessons, four separate pieces: sync costs roughly 3x async (Lesson 1), the same key routes to the same partition on trip data as long as its encoding stays consistent (Lesson 2), acks/batching/compression each trade something for something (Lesson 3), and two different failure classes need two different handling strategies (Lesson 4). This lesson assembles all four into one producer that does something CityFlow’s analytics pipeline would actually need: replay historical trip data onto Kafka as if it were happening live.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Filter trip data down to genuinely valid timestamps before using it
  • Pace a replay using actual inter-arrival gaps, scaled to a demo-friendly speed
  • Produce trip events keyed by pickup zone, with acks and error handling chosen deliberately
  • Verify a replay’s correctness by consuming everything back and checking it

Real Data Has Real Problems: Filtering First#

Read January 2024’s trip data and check the pickup timestamps before doing anything else with them:

18 rows, out of nearly 3 million, carry a pickup timestamp that isn’t actually in January 2024 at all — a handful land in 2002, others in 2009, almost certainly a taxi meter’s clock being wrong at the moment a trip started, a small-scale data quality issue rather than a corrupted file. Skipping this filter wouldn’t crash anything — the rows are otherwise well-formed — but it would badly break the next step: sorting by pickup time and taking the earliest rows would pull in a trip from 2002 as if it happened before every trip on New Year’s Day 2024, and the pacing this lesson builds next depends entirely on gaps between genuinely consecutive pickups.

Pacing a Replay from Actual Arrival Gaps#

Sort the filtered data by pickup time, take the first 150 trips, and compute the gap between each one and the trip before it:

These 150 trips, all from the quiet early-morning hours of January 1st, 2024, span about 455 seconds — under 8 minutes — with gaps between consecutive trips ranging from under a second to about 13 seconds. Replaying them at their actual pace would make this guided project take nearly 8 minutes to demonstrate; replaying them instantly would defeat the entire point of a paced replay. The middle ground: compress elapsed time by a fixed factor, with a cap so no single unusually large gap stalls the demo:

SPEED = 15 means every second of gap becomes a fifteenth of a second of actual sleep — CityFlow’s morning traffic, replayed 15 times faster than it really happened, still preserving the relative pacing (a 13-second gap stays noticeably longer than a 1-second gap) without the demo taking minutes. MAX_GAP exists for the same reason Lesson 5 of Module 1 needed wait_until_topic() instead of a blind sleep: trip data occasionally has genuinely large gaps (a quiet stretch overnight), and without a cap, one long gap would stall an otherwise fast demo for no pedagogical benefit.

Producing With Every Lesson’s Choices Applied#

Construct the producer with acks='all' (Lesson 3: CityFlow’s trip-completion events are billing-relevant, worth the throughput cost for the durability guarantee), a consistent key_serializer (Lesson 2: the same encoding, every time, for every zone), some batching headroom (linger_ms=20, Lesson 3), and both classes of error handling from Lesson 4:

Two separate failure paths are live here, exactly matching Lesson 4’s two classes: the try/except KafkaTimeoutError around producer.send(...) itself catches a Class 1 failure (say, cityflow-trip-events somehow not existing), while future.add_errback(on_error) catches a Class 2 failure per message (a broker-side rejection after the send was accepted) without ever stopping the loop — one bad trip event doesn’t take down the replay of the other 149. Neither path fired this run, which the errors during replay: 0 line confirms explicitly rather than leaving to assumption.

Verifying the Replay, Not Just Trusting It#

A replay that reports success isn’t the same as a replay that’s actually correct. Consume every message back and check three things: the count, the zone-to-partition consistency Lesson 2 proved, and an aggregate number nothing here was told in advance:

150 sent, 150 consumed back — nothing lost, nothing duplicated. 48 distinct zones, zero split across more than one partition, the exact same guarantee Lesson 2 proved now holding on a paced replay rather than a batch produced all at once. And $4,931.86 — a number this verification script never hardcoded anywhere; it’s the honest sum of total_amount across whichever 150 rows happened to be the earliest-timestamped trips in the filtered January data, recomputed from what Kafka actually stored and handed back, not from the source DataFrame still sitting in memory.

Practice Exercises#

Exercise 1: Replay a later window of the month#

Change the filter to select trips starting from, say, 2024-01-15 08:00:00 instead of the very start of the month (rush hour on a weekday, not 3 minutes past midnight), and replay 150 trips from there instead. Compare the gap statistics — are consecutive trips closer together during a January weekday morning than they were at this lesson’s own quiet midnight window? What does that imply about how many partitions’ worth of parallel consumers CityFlow would realistically need during rush hour versus overnight?

Exercise 2: Scale up and measure whether acks='all' still holds#

Increase N_TRIPS to 1000 and re-run the full replay. Does errors during replay stay at 0? Does the total elapsed time scale roughly linearly with the pacing, or does something else dominate at this larger scale? Report both numbers.

Hint

At N_TRIPS = 1000, gaps stop being uniformly small the way this lesson’s first 150 trips were — a weekday’s worth of trips spans hours, so MAX_GAP’s 1-second cap will bind far more often, and the total replay time will be driven mostly by however many gaps get capped, not by the underlying pacing.

Summary#

Every lesson in this module fed into one producer: filtering caught 18 corrupt timestamps before they could corrupt the sort pacing depends on, SPEED/MAX_GAP turned an 8-minute replay into a 19-second demo without losing relative pacing, acks='all' and a consistent key_serializer applied Lesson 2 and Lesson 3’s justified choices rather than defaults, and both of Lesson 4’s error-handling paths ran live, catching nothing because nothing failed — confirmed explicitly, not assumed. Consuming every message back proved the replay correct on three independent axes: exact count, zone-to-partition consistency, and an honestly recomputed revenue total.

Key Concepts#

  • Data needs filtering before it can be trusted for pacing — 18 corrupt timestamps, found and dropped before sorting, out of nearly 3 million rows.
  • Pacing can be scaled without losing its relative shape — a 15x speedup with a capped maximum gap kept relative timing intact in a fraction of the time.
  • Every prior lesson’s decision gets applied together, deliberately — acks, key_serializer, batching, and two-class error handling, not defaults.
  • A replay is only proven correct by consuming it back and checking, not by a “produced successfully” message alone — count, partition consistency, and an independently recomputed total all confirmed here.

Why This Matters#

This module’s producer is what everything from here writes through. Module 4’s consumer groups read from cityflow-trip-events next, and its own guided project reconciles what a consumer group actually receives against Course 1’s already-established numbers — a reconciliation that only means something because this lesson’s replay producer is provably correct first. Getting the producer right, with verification instead of a hopeful print statement, is what makes every later module’s numbers trustworthy.
