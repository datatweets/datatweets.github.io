Welcome to Inside the XGBoost Objective#

In Module 1 you built a gradient booster by hand. Each tree was fit to the pseudo-residual, the negative gradient of the loss, and added to the model with a learning rate. In Lesson 1 you saw XGBoost beat plain gradient boosting on the same California Housing split, 0.4696 to 0.5422 on test RMSE. This lesson shows why. The reason is not a better tree-growing trick bolted onto the same idea. It is a different objective and a smarter way of approximating it.

XGBoost does two things Module 1’s booster did not. First, it adds a regularization term directly to the objective, so the algorithm is not just chasing training loss but also paying a price for complex trees. Second, it uses a second-order (Newton) approximation of the loss: it uses the gradient and also the hessian, the second derivative of the loss at each point. Three results fall out of those two ingredients: a closed form for the best value to put in each leaf, a score that measures how good a leaf is, and the gain XGBoost uses to decide where to split and whether a split is worth keeping. We will derive each piece, then check the numbers against a one-tree XGBoost fit and watch the math and the library agree to the decimal.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Write down XGBoost’s regularized objective and explain the roles of γ \gamma and λ \lambda
  • Expand the loss to second order and identify the gradient gi g_i and hessian hi h_i of each observation
  • Derive the optimal leaf weight wj∗=−Gj/(Hj+λ) w_j^* = -G_j / (H_j + \lambda) by minimizing the per-leaf objective
  • Compute the leaf similarity score Gj2/(Hj+λ) G_j^2 / (H_j + \lambda) and the split gain that XGBoost maximizes
  • Explain how γ \gamma sets a minimum gain and prunes splits, and verify every quantity against a real xgboost fit

You should be comfortable with the pseudo-residual idea from Module 1, basic numpy, and reading a derivative and a second derivative. Let’s begin.

The Regularized Objective#

Module 1’s booster only ever tried to shrink the training loss. XGBoost minimizes a larger quantity that adds a penalty for model complexity. Written across all K K trees in the ensemble, the objective is

obj=∑iL(yi,y^i)+∑k=1KΩ(fk) \text{obj} = \sum_{i} L(y_i, \hat{y}_i) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \Omega(f_k)

The first sum is the familiar training loss over all observations. The second sum is new: for every tree fk f_k in the model, XGBoost adds a regularization term Ω \Omega that grows with how complex that tree is. For a single tree with T T leaves and leaf weights w1,…,wT w_1, \dots, w_T , the penalty is

Ω(f)=γ T+12λ∑j=1Twj2 \Omega(f) = \gamma\, T + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{T} w_j^2

Read the two pieces separately. The term γ T \gamma\, T charges a flat cost γ \gamma for each leaf the tree has, so a bushier tree pays more; this is what discourages needless splits and, as we will see, powers pruning. The term 12λ∑jwj2 \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_j w_j^2 is an L2 penalty on the leaf weights, exactly like ridge regression, pulling every leaf value toward zero so no single leaf can make an extreme prediction. The 12 \tfrac{1}{2} is a convention that makes the derivative clean, and λ \lambda (called reg_lambda in the library) controls how hard the shrinkage pulls. Neither penalty exists in a plain gradient booster, and together they are the first reason XGBoost generalizes so well.

Regularization lives in the objective, not as an afterthought

In many models regularization is a separate step you tune around the edges. In XGBoost, γ \gamma and λ \lambda sit inside the objective the tree-builder optimizes. That means every leaf weight and every split decision is already made with the penalty in view, rather than fitting an unpenalized tree and shrinking it later. This is why you will see λ \lambda appear directly in the leaf-weight formula below, not as a post-processing knob.

Second-Order Approximation: Gradients and Hessians#

Here is the idea before the algebra. To add the next tree, XGBoost has to guess how the loss will change if it nudges each prediction. Module 1 guessed using only the slope of the loss (the gradient), the way plain gradient descent does. XGBoost also uses the curvature of the loss (the hessian), the way Newton’s method does, so it can size each step better instead of leaning entirely on a hand-set learning rate.

MethodUsesBoosting version
Gradient descentslope only (gradient)Module 1’s booster
Newton’s methodslope and curvature (gradient and hessian)XGBoost

With that picture in place, here is the math it comes from. When XGBoost adds the next tree ft f_t , the new prediction for observation i i becomes y^i(t−1)+ft(xi) \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)} + f_t(x_i) . Plugging that into the loss and expanding with a second-order Taylor series around the current prediction y^i(t−1) \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)} gives, for each observation,

L ⁣(yi, y^i(t−1)+ft(xi))≈L ⁣(yi, y^i(t−1))+gi ft(xi)+12hi ft(xi)2 L\!\left(y_i,\, \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)} + f_t(x_i)\right) \approx L\!\left(y_i,\, \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)}\right) + g_i\, f_t(x_i) + \tfrac{1}{2} h_i\, f_t(x_i)^2

Two new quantities appear. The gradient is the first derivative of the loss with respect to the current prediction, and the hessian is the second derivative:

gi=∂L(yi,y^)∂y^∣y^=y^i(t−1),hi=∂2L(yi,y^)∂y^2∣y^=y^i(t−1) g_i = \frac{\partial L(y_i, \hat{y})}{\partial \hat{y}} \Bigg|_{\hat{y} = \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)}}, \qquad h_i = \frac{\partial^2 L(y_i, \hat{y})}{\partial \hat{y}^2} \Bigg|_{\hat{y} = \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)}}

Module 1 used only the gradient gi g_i , so it is a first-order method. XGBoost keeps the second-order term 12hift(xi)2 \tfrac{1}{2} h_i f_t(x_i)^2 as well, which is the Newton step from the table above. For squared-error loss L=12(y−y^)2 L = \tfrac{1}{2}(y - \hat{y})^2 , the two derivatives are especially simple:

gi=∂∂y^12(yi−y^)2=y^i−yi,hi=∂∂y^(y^i−yi)=1 g_i = \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{y}} \tfrac{1}{2}(y_i - \hat{y})^2 = \hat{y}_i - y_i, \qquad h_i = \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{y}} (\hat{y}_i - y_i) = 1

The gradient is the (signed) prediction error, and the hessian is exactly 1 1 for every point. Let’s build a tiny Northwind dataset and compute these directly. Imagine six orders with a units-sold target, and a starting prediction equal to XGBoost’s default base_score of 0.5.

Each gradient is 0.5 - y, a large negative number because the base prediction of 0.5 sits far below every real units figure, telling the tree those predictions must rise. Every hessian is 1.0, as the derivation promised. These two vectors, g g and h h , are the only things about the loss that the rest of the algorithm ever sees. Everything below is built purely from sums of g g and h h .

The Optimal Leaf Weight#

Now put the tree structure into the picture. A tree assigns each observation to exactly one leaf, and every observation in leaf j j receives the same predicted value, the leaf weight wj w_j . Substituting the second-order expansion into the regularized objective and dropping the constant L(yi,y^(t−1)) L(y_i, \hat{y}^{(t-1)}) terms (they do not depend on the new tree), the part of the objective that leaf j j contributes is

objj=(∑i∈jgi)wj+12(∑i∈jhi+λ)wj2 \text{obj}_j = \left(\sum_{i \in j} g_i\right) w_j + \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i \in j} h_i + \lambda\right) w_j^2

Notice the λ \lambda that appears next to the summed hessians: it comes straight from the L2 penalty 12λwj2 \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda w_j^2 in Ω \Omega . To keep the notation clean, define the leaf’s summed gradient and summed hessian as

Gj=∑i∈jgi,Hj=∑i∈jhi G_j = \sum_{i \in j} g_i, \qquad H_j = \sum_{i \in j} h_i

The per-leaf objective is now a simple upward parabola in wj w_j : objj=Gj wj+12(Hj+λ) wj2 \text{obj}_j = G_j\, w_j + \tfrac{1}{2}(H_j + \lambda)\, w_j^2 . Minimize it by setting the derivative with respect to wj w_j to zero, Gj+(Hj+λ) wj=0 G_j + (H_j + \lambda)\, w_j = 0 , which gives the optimal leaf weight

wj∗=−∑i∈jgi∑i∈jhi+λ=−GjHj+λ w_j^* = -\frac{\sum_{i \in j} g_i}{\sum_{i \in j} h_i + \lambda} = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda}

This is a closed form. XGBoost does not search for the best leaf value; it computes it in one division. The λ \lambda in the denominator is the shrinkage: raise it and every leaf weight moves toward zero. Let’s compute the optimal weight for the case where all six orders land in a single leaf.

With every order in one leaf, the best single prediction to add on top of the 0.5 base is 18.4286, so the leaf would push predictions up to roughly 18.93, near the middle of the units values. Try raising reg_lambda and you will watch this weight shrink toward zero: at λ=10 \lambda = 10 it drops to about 8.06, and at λ=100 \lambda = 100 to about 1.22. That is L2 regularization acting directly through the denominator, exactly as the formula predicts.

The Similarity Score: How Good Is a Leaf?#

We have the best weight for a leaf. How good is that leaf, in objective terms? Substitute wj∗ w_j^* back into the per-leaf objective objj=Gjwj+12(Hj+λ)wj2 \text{obj}_j = G_j w_j + \tfrac{1}{2}(H_j + \lambda) w_j^2 . After the algebra, the minimized value collapses to a single clean expression:

objj∗=−12 Gj2Hj+λ \text{obj}_j^* = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{G_j^2}{H_j + \lambda}

This is the smallest (most negative, hence best) contribution leaf j j can make to the objective. Because a more negative objective is better, XGBoost thinks of the bracketed quantity, stripped of the −12 -\tfrac{1}{2} , as a positive-is-better similarity score (also called the leaf quality or structure score):

similarityj=Gj2Hj+λ \text{similarity}_j = \frac{G_j^2}{H_j + \lambda}

The name “similarity” is apt: the numerator Gj2=(∑i∈jgi)2 G_j^2 = \left(\sum_{i \in j} g_i\right)^2 is large when the residuals in the leaf all point the same way and reinforce each other, and small when they cancel out. A leaf full of observations that agree on which direction the prediction should move earns a high similarity score; a leaf mixing positive and negative residuals earns a low one, because the summed gradient nearly cancels. Compute the similarity of our single all-six-rows leaf.

The root leaf, holding all six orders, scores 2377.2857. On its own that number means little; its whole purpose is to be compared against the similarity of the two leaves we would get by splitting it. That comparison is the gain.

The Gain: Choosing and Pruning Splits#

Every candidate split takes a parent leaf and divides its observations into a left child and a right child. XGBoost decides whether the split is worthwhile by asking: does splitting raise the total similarity, and by enough to justify the γ \gamma cost of adding a leaf? Writing GL,HL G_L, H_L for the left child’s sums, GR,HR G_R, H_R for the right child’s, and noting the parent’s sums are GL+GR G_L + G_R and HL+HR H_L + H_R , the gain of the split is

Gain=12[GL2HL+λ+GR2HR+λ−(GL+GR)2HL+HR+λ]−γ \text{Gain} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L + \lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R + \lambda} - \frac{(G_L + G_R)^2}{H_L + H_R + \lambda}\right] - \gamma

In words: gain is one half of (left similarity plus right similarity minus parent similarity), minus the per-leaf penalty γ \gamma . The bracket measures how much cleaner the two children are than the single parent; the γ \gamma is the toll for the extra leaf. XGBoost evaluates this for every candidate split point and picks the one with the largest gain. Crucially, it keeps a split only if its gain is positive. If the best available split has gain ≤0 \le 0 , that branch is not grown; the two children would not improve the penalized objective enough to pay for themselves. This is exactly how γ \gamma (the gamma hyperparameter) prunes: raising γ \gamma raises the bar every split must clear, so fewer splits survive and trees stay smaller.

Let’s evaluate a real split on our six orders. The natural cut separates the three low-units orders from the three high-units orders.

The split earns a gain of 76.42, comfortably positive, so XGBoost keeps it. The two children have leaf weights 8.625 (the low-units orders) and 23.625 (the high-units orders), and their combined similarity (297.56 + 2232.56 = 2530.12) exceeds the parent’s 2377.29, which is what makes the gain positive. For contrast, an “interleaved” split that scatters low and high orders on both sides mixes opposite-signed residuals in each child, so its similarity barely rises above the parent and its gain comes out negative (about -123.58), a split XGBoost would refuse to make. Verifying against an XGBoost fit#

Hand math is only convincing if the library agrees. Let’s fit a one-tree XGBoost model on the same six orders, with the same reg_lambda, gamma, and base_score, and read off its leaf values with get_dump. To make the leaf weights appear directly (rather than shrunk by a learning rate), we set eta=1.0.

Everything lines up. XGBoost split at f0 < 2.5, exactly separating the three low-units orders from the three high-units orders, and its leaf values are 8.625 and 23.625, matching our hand-computed wL∗ w_L^* and wR∗ w_R^* to the digit. The predictions are those leaf weights added to the base_score of 0.5, giving 9.125 and 24.125, which is why understanding base_score as the starting prediction F F matters. One caveat about the reported gain=152.84: XGBoost prints the gain without the outer 12 \tfrac{1}{2} factor, and indeed 2×76.42=152.84 2 \times 76.42 = 152.84 . The value the library compares against gamma internally is this un-halved bracket; the 12 \tfrac{1}{2} is a constant that does not change which split wins, so both conventions rank splits identically.

What “cover” means in the dump

Look at cover=6 on the root and cover=3 on each leaf. For squared-error loss every hessian is 1, so Hj H_j is simply the number of rows in the leaf, which is exactly what “cover” reports. For other losses the hessian is not 1 (for log loss it is p(1−p) p(1-p) ), so cover becomes the summed hessian rather than a plain row count. Lesson 3’s min_child_weight knob is a floor on this cover.

Practice Exercises#

Try these before checking the hints. They exercise the leaf-weight, similarity, and gain formulas you just derived.

Exercise 1: Watch Regularization Shrink the Leaf Weight#

Watch the L2 penalty pull a leaf weight toward zero as λ \lambda grows.

  • Start from the six orders’ summed gradient G = -129.0 and summed hessian H = 6.0.
  • For each reg_lambda in 0.0, 1.0, 10.0, 100.0, compute w∗=−G/(H+λ) w^* = -G/(H + \lambda) .
  • Print each λ \lambda next to its weight.
  • Explain in one sentence what happens to the weight as λ \lambda grows, and why.

Hint

Compute w = -G / (H + lam) inside the loop. You should get about 21.5, 18.43, 8.06, and 1.22. As λ \lambda grows, the denominator grows, so the weight shrinks steadily toward zero. That is the L2 penalty from Ω \Omega acting directly through the leaf-weight formula, damping how extreme any single leaf’s prediction can be.

Exercise 2: Find the Gamma That Prunes a Split#

Find the gamma large enough to prune the good split on the six orders.

  • Take the halved gain of 76.42 at gamma = 0 (the similarity rise 2530.125 - 2377.2857, halved).
  • Print the gain (half_rise - gamma) for gamma values of 0, 50, and 100.
  • Identify the smallest γ \gamma that makes the gain non-positive, so XGBoost would prune the split.

Hint

Gain is half_rise - gamma, so it turns non-positive once gamma exceeds 76.42. At gamma = 50 the gain is still positive (26.42, kept); at gamma = 100 it is negative (-23.58, pruned). This is precisely how the gamma hyperparameter controls tree size: it is the minimum gain a split must produce to be allowed, so larger gamma means more aggressive pruning and simpler trees.

Exercise 3: Gradients and Hessians for Log Loss#

Squared-error loss has the simplest possible hessian, hi=1 h_i = 1 . Log loss does not.

  • Use the log-loss formulas with respect to the raw score: g=p−y g = p - y and h=p(1−p) h = p(1 - p) .
  • For three points with label y = 1 and predicted probabilities p = [0.5, 0.9, 0.99], compute g and h.
  • Note how the hessian changes as the predicted probability approaches the label.

Hint

You get gradients [-0.5, -0.1, -0.01] and hessians [0.25, 0.09, 0.0099]. As the predicted probability approaches the correct label, both the gradient and the hessian shrink toward zero. The vanishing hessian is why confident, correct points contribute almost nothing to a leaf’s cover under log loss, and it is exactly the curvature information that a first-order booster (Module 1) throws away but XGBoost’s Newton step uses.

Summary#

You have seen precisely what makes XGBoost different from the hand-built booster of Module 1. It optimizes a regularized objective, and it approximates that objective to second order, using both the gradient and the hessian of the loss. From those two choices, a closed-form leaf weight, a leaf similarity score, and a split gain all fall out, and an XGBoost fit matches them exactly.

Key Concepts#

Regularized Objective

  • XGBoost minimizes ∑iL(yi,y^i)+∑kΩ(fk) \sum_i L(y_i, \hat{y}_i) + \sum_k \Omega(f_k) with Ω(f)=γT+12λ∑jwj2 \Omega(f) = \gamma T + \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_j w_j^2
  • γ \gamma charges a flat cost per leaf (controls pruning); λ \lambda is an L2 penalty on leaf weights (controls shrinkage)

Gradients and Hessians

  • A second-order Taylor expansion introduces gi=∂y^L g_i = \partial_{\hat{y}} L and hi=∂y^2L h_i = \partial^2_{\hat{y}} L
  • For squared error, gi=y^i−yi g_i = \hat{y}_i - y_i and hi=1 h_i = 1 ; the algorithm sees the loss only through sums of g g and h h

Optimal Leaf Weight

  • wj∗=−Gj/(Hj+λ) w_j^* = -G_j / (H_j + \lambda) is a closed form, not a search: XGBoost computes each leaf value in one division
  • Raising λ \lambda shrinks every weight toward zero through the denominator

Similarity and Gain

  • Leaf quality is the similarity score Gj2/(Hj+λ) G_j^2 / (H_j + \lambda) ; residuals that agree raise it, residuals that cancel keep it low
  • Gain=12[ GL2/(HL+λ)+GR2/(HR+λ)−(GL+GR)2/(HL+HR+λ) ]−γ \text{Gain} = \tfrac{1}{2}[\, G_L^2/(H_L+\lambda) + G_R^2/(H_R+\lambda) - (G_L+G_R)^2/(H_L+H_R+\lambda)\,] - \gamma
  • A split is kept only if its gain is positive, so γ \gamma sets the minimum gain and prunes

The library computes exactly this

  • A one-tree fit’s leaf values match −G/(H+λ) -G/(H+\lambda) to the digit, and its predictions are those weights added to base_score
  • XGBoost’s reported gain omits the outer 12 \tfrac{1}{2} , so it prints twice the halved value; the ranking of splits is unchanged

Why This Matters#

Every hyperparameter you will meet in the next lesson connects back to a symbol in this objective. reg_lambda is the λ \lambda in the leaf-weight denominator; gamma is the γ \gamma that a split’s gain must beat; max_depth and min_child_weight bound how the gain-maximizing search is allowed to grow trees. Because you derived the formulas and watched the library reproduce them to the decimal, those knobs are no longer mysterious dials. When Northwind’s model overfits, you will know that raising reg_lambda shrinks leaf weights and raising gamma refuses low-gain splits, because you have seen both effects in the algebra and in the numbers.

This objective is also the shared foundation of the whole gradient-boosting family. LightGBM and CatBoost use the same Newton step with gradients and hessians and the same similarity-based split scoring; they differ mainly in how they search for splits, not in what a split is worth. Master this one derivation and you understand the scoring engine at the heart of every modern boosting library.

Continue Building Your Skills#

You have just derived the mathematical core of XGBoost and watched a real fit confirm it to the decimal. The regularized objective, the gradient and hessian, the closed-form leaf weight −G/(H+λ) -G/(H+\lambda) , the similarity score G2/(H+λ) G^2/(H+\lambda) , and the gain that chooses and prunes splits are not five separate tricks; they are one objective seen from five angles. Hold onto the picture in the figure: sum the gradients and hessians, turn them into a similarity, and let the rise in similarity decide the split. Every hyperparameter, every “why did it overfit,” and every rival library in the rest of this course maps back to that one idea. Learn it once here, from the math and the numbers together, and XGBoost will never again feel like a black box.
