Welcome to the Guided Project#

Every tool this module built comes together here: concatenation’s lesson that row counts must be checked, not assumed; every join type’s verified row-count behavior; non-equality matching for a date-range condition. This guided project combines all six real Thistlewood tables – customers, orders, order_items, products, returns, and promotions – into one unified dataset, using a check_join() diagnostic function that checks the row count before and after every single join, so growth or shrinkage is caught the moment it happens, not discovered later in a wrong total.

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Build a join-quality diagnostic function that classifies row-count changes and flags unexpected ones
  • Run a five-step join pipeline combining every Thistlewood table, checked at every step
  • Reconcile a unified dataset’s revenue against an independently-established grand total
  • Explain why a gross-revenue figure and a net-of-returns figure are two different numbers, not a mismatch to force into agreement

The Diagnostic Function#

check_join() takes a before-frame and an after-frame, computes the row-count delta, and classifies it as unchanged, grew, or shrank – then compares that observation against what the step was expected to do, so a join that grows when it should have stayed the same size (a fan-out bug) or shrinks when every row should have matched (a dropped-row bug) gets flagged immediately, in the diagnostic table itself, instead of surfacing later as a wrong number three steps downstream.

Step 1-3: Customers, Order Items, Products#

Three joins, three expectations, checked as they happen: orders to customers should stay unchanged (every order already has a matching customer, confirmed back in Lesson 2), step1 to items should grow (one order maps to 1-4 line items, an expected fan-out), and step2 to products should stay unchanged (every product_id in items exists in products, and product_id is unique in products, so no further fan-out and no drop).

Step 4-5: Returns and Active Promotions#

Step 4 renames returns’ own quantity to return_quantity first – items already has a quantity column, and joining without renaming would silently create a quantity_right, an easy-to-miss naming collision. (order_id, product_id) is the composite key here (Lesson 4 verified no return repeats the same order-item pair), so the join stays unchanged.

Step 5 reuses Lesson 4’s non-equality join_where() to find which line items were bought under an active category promotion, then joins that small match table back with an ordinary left join – exactly the pattern Lesson 4 recommended for keeping every row while join_where() itself stays inner-only. Before joining it back, dup_matches confirms no order-item row matches more than one active promotion (Lesson 4 already found no overlapping category promo windows), so this step is expected to stay unchanged too – and does.

The Diagnostic Report#

Every one of the 5 join steps landed exactly where it was expected to: one expected fan-out at step 2 (6,000 orders to 14,054 line items – the same order-to-item relationship every earlier module has worked with), and no unexpected growth or shrinkage anywhere else. The unified dataset ends at exactly 14,054 rows – matching thistlewood_order_items.csv’s own row count exactly, proof this pipeline attached customer, product, return, and promotion context onto every order-item row without ever losing or duplicating one.

An UNEXPECTED flag is the diagnostic doing its job

Every step here happened to land on OK because this dataset’s keys are clean. If step 4’s join had used only order_id instead of the composite (order_id, product_id) key, an order with more than one returned line item would fan out step 4’s row count – check_join() would report expected="unchanged" against an observed="grew", flag UNEXPECTED, and point straight at the join that needs fixing, before the mistake ever reached the revenue reconciliation below.

Revenue Reconciliation#

The unified dataset’s line_total column sums to the exact $9,195,043.62 grand total this course established in Module 7 – expected, since every join in this pipeline only added columns from customers, products, returns, and promotions onto the existing order-item rows; none of them touched line_total itself or changed how many order-item rows exist.

What the Grand Total Doesn’t Include#

The $9,195,043.62 grand total is a gross figure: line_total records what each line item sold for at the time of sale, and joining returns onto the unified dataset doesn’t reduce it – it only attaches the fact that a return later happened. Multiplying each returned row’s return_quantity by its own unit_price_at_sale gives $200,232.03 in refunded revenue, about 2.18% of the gross total. Net-of-returns revenue – $8,994,811.59 – is a different number from the $9,195,043.62 grand total, and this lesson doesn’t force them to match, because they measure two different things on purpose: gross is “what was sold,” net is “what was ultimately kept.” A join-quality diagnostic proves the pipeline didn’t introduce an error; it was never meant to make gross revenue and net revenue the same figure.

Promotions are informational here, not re-applied

This unified dataset’s promo_discount_pct column shows which category promotion (if any) was active when a line item sold, but line_total is never re-discounted using it. Lesson 4’s check already found that unit_price_at_sale for line items bought during an active promotion runs roughly 70-90% of the product’s list unit_price – the discount is already baked into the historical sale price. Applying promo_discount_pct a second time on top of line_total would double-count a discount that’s already there.

Practice Exercises#

Exercise 1: Add a “returned” flag and confirm segment behavior#

Add pl.col("return_id").is_not_null().alias("was_returned") to unified, then group_by("segment").agg(pl.col("was_returned").mean().round(4).alias("return_rate")). Confirm the result has exactly 2 rows (retail, wholesale) and report which segment returns more often.

Exercise 2: Break check_join() on purpose, then read its own flag#

Rebuild step 4 using only on="order_id" instead of the composite ["order_id", "product_id"] key, re-run check_join() with expected="unchanged", and confirm it reports observed="grew" and flag="UNEXPECTED". Explain in one sentence why dropping product_id from the join key causes fan-out here.

Summary#

check_join() ran across a five-step pipeline joining all six Thistlewood tables – customers, orders, order_items, products, returns, and promotions – and flagged every one of the 5 steps OK: one expected fan-out (6,000 orders to 14,054 line items) and four steps that correctly stayed unchanged. The unified dataset’s gross revenue matched the module’s established $9,195,043.62 grand total exactly, and a follow-up reconciled the separate question of returns: $200,232.03 refunded (2.18% of gross), landing on a different $8,994,811.59 net-of-returns figure – reported as its own number, not forced to match the gross total it was never meant to equal.

Key Concepts#

  • A join-quality diagnostic checks row counts before and after every step, classifies the change, and flags anything that doesn’t match what that specific join was expected to do – catching a fan-out or a silent drop exactly where it happens.
  • A composite join key matters when a single-column key would be ambiguous – (order_id, product_id) for returns avoided a fan-out that order_id alone would have caused.
  • A unified dataset’s total should still reconcile to an independently-known number – this project’s $9,195,043.62 match is that same discipline, applied across a five-table join instead of a single aggregation.
  • Gross revenue and net-of-returns revenue are two different questions, not a discrepancy to resolve – a correct pipeline reports both rather than forcing one to match the other.

Why This Matters#

A “unified dataset” that silently drops or duplicates rows during its own construction is a common source of wrong downstream numbers – and the fix isn’t more careful joins, it’s checking every join the way this lesson’s check_join() did: a before/after count, an expectation, a flag the moment they disagree. This module’s own $9,195,043.62 grand total, reconciled here for the first time across a multi-table join pipeline rather than a single group_by(), is the concrete proof that concatenation, every join type, non-equality matching, and pivoting all combine correctly when each step is verified on its own terms.
