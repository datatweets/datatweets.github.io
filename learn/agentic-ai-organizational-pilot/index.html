Overview

From interest to a decision-ready pilot

Most organizations don't have an agentic AI problem — they have a follow-through problem. A workflow gets identified, a demo gets built, someone asks "is this actually worth doing," and the trail goes cold because no one produced the evidence that question needs: a real baseline, an honest cost range, a security and governance plan, and a bounded way to test it that a skeptical stakeholder would sign off on.

This course is built around that gap. Each day adds one piece of a working AI Agent Pilot Dossier — not a slide deck of possibilities, but the actual artifacts a pilot decision requires: an opportunity definition, a business case, a platform decision, an integration map, a risk register, KPIs and decision gates, a governance plan, and an organizational-impact review. By Day 14 you assemble them into one executive-ready adoption roadmap.

The method is deliberately narrow: one real or plausible workflow, carried through all 14 days, rather than fourteen disconnected topics. That's what makes the dossier coherent instead of a folder of unrelated worksheets.

The gap

Why agentic AI efforts stall before a real pilot

Three questions show up in almost every stalled initiative. If you can't answer all three for your own idea right now, that's exactly what this course is for.

Question 01

Unclear value

Can you state the baseline this would improve, in a number, and the range of value you honestly expect — including the case where it isn't worth building?

Question 02

Disconnected technology

Does anyone know exactly which systems, data, and people this would touch, and where a human has to approve before anything executes?

Question 03

Weak governance and ownership

If this went wrong tomorrow, is there a named owner, a rollback plan, and a review trigger — or would the first incident be the first time anyone asked?

The deliverable

What you'll build: the AI Agent Pilot Dossier

Fourteen daily artifacts are the working pieces. Day 14 doesn't introduce a new assignment — it has you organize them into these ten final components, then write a short executive adoption roadmap ending in one explicit recommendation: continue, change, or stop.

  1. 1

    Strategic framing and opportunity definition

    The workflow you're examining, its autonomy boundary, and the opportunity you're pursuing (Days 1–2).

  2. 2

    Business case, baseline, and uncertainty

    Costs, expected value as a range, and the assumptions behind it (Day 4).

  3. 3

    Platform and architecture decision

    A weighted, defensible platform choice — not a vendor popularity contest (Day 3).

  4. 4

    Workflow, ecosystem, and integration design

    The current-state map plus how the agent connects to systems, tools, and people (Days 5–6).

  5. 5

    Security and information-integrity plan

    A threat-and-control register plus an incident playbook (Days 7–8).

  6. 6

    Functional portfolio and operating model

    Where else this could apply, and who owns what once it's running (Days 9–10).

  7. 7

    Bounded pilot charter

    Scope, authority limits, baseline, monitoring, and exit criteria (Day 11).

  8. 8

    KPIs, tests, and decision rules

    The measures and thresholds that turn a demo into evidence (Day 12).

  9. 9

    Governance and accountability plan

    Risk tier, decision rights, controls, and review triggers (Day 13).

  10. 10

    Organizational-impact review and adoption roadmap

    Who benefits, who bears risk, and a continue/change/stop recommendation (Day 14).

Self-assessment rubric

Before calling the dossier done, check it against all nine:

Strategic fit
Does the opportunity map to a real organizational priority, not a novelty?
Evidence
Is the baseline and expected value grounded in stated assumptions, not a guess dressed up as a number?
Integration realism
Does the workflow map name the actual systems and people involved?
Safety / security
Does the threat register cover the risks specific to this use case, not a generic list?
Accountability
Is there a named owner for the pilot and for its risks?
Human impact
Does it say honestly whose work changes, and how?
Reversibility
Is there a genuine rollback path, not just a plan for success?
Measurement quality
Do the KPIs actually distinguish "working" from "not working"?
Clarity of recommendation
Does it end in one explicit continue/change/stop call, not a hedge?

Where it applies

Applications across business functions

Hypothetical, composite illustrations — not case studies or promised results. One rule applies to all eight: a human reviews or approves before anything executes, never "draft" upgraded to "auto-send" without someone deciding that. Acceptable autonomy still differs sharply by function, which is why several of these deliberately stay at "draft" or "recommend."

OperationsDraft exception-handling responses for a logistics queue.
ProductSummarize and cluster user feedback into candidate themes.
Customer experienceDraft support replies grounded in documentation, with citations.
Knowledge workRetrieve and summarize internal policy documents for a first-pass answer.
Software / dataDraft a first-pass root-cause note from logs and recent deploys.
MarketingGenerate on-brand content variants for an editor to choose from.
Finance / procurementFlag invoice or expense anomalies. High-stakes financial decisions stay outside a bounded pilot's authority entirely.
People operationsDraft onboarding-document answers. Employment, compensation, and disciplinary decisions are explicitly out of scope for a first pilot.
